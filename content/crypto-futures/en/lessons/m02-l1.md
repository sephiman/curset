# What an exchange is and who holds your coins

An exchange is where you turn one asset into another: dollars into bitcoin, bitcoin into a stablecoin. Opening an account and placing an order feels familiar, but the custody model is fundamentally different from traditional banking. While your coins sit on an exchange, the exchange holds them. Your balance is a record in the venue's database, not coins in your own wallet. Understanding who controls the funds at each step determines whether your capital survives unexpected venue failures.

## Centralised vs decentralised: CEX and DEX

A centralised exchange (CEX) is a private company. You register an account, pass identity checks, and deposit funds into wallets managed by the company. The exchange matches your buy against someone else's sell on its internal order book.

Matching trades off-chain on proprietary servers allows microsecond execution and low transaction fees. If you forget your credentials, customer support can reset access. That convenience comes with the risk of trusting one operator. The moment you deposit, your coins move into the exchange's custody. Your on-screen balance is an IOU. If the company mismanages reserves or freezes withdrawals during market turmoil, your balance is locked.

A decentralised exchange (DEX) operates through smart contracts deployed directly on a blockchain. You trade directly from your own self-custody wallet, and transactions settle on-chain.

Smart contracts remove corporate intermediaries, meaning no company can freeze your account or deny a withdrawal. That independence removes the safety net. Transactions are irreversible; approving a malicious contract or sending funds to the wrong address cannot be undone. Traditional markets pause when the closing bell rings, but both CEX and DEX markets operate continuously around the clock.

:::note{type=info}
A CEX concentrates risk in a single operating company. A DEX shifts execution risk entirely onto you and the smart contract code. Beginners often start on a CEX for liquidity and convenience, but leaving long-term capital there turns custody into uncompensated credit risk.
:::

## Spot trading: owning the asset itself

Spot trading means buying or selling the asset itself with immediate settlement. You pay currency now, and you receive the coin now.

Spot contrasts directly with the leveraged perpetual futures covered later. In spot, your balance represents physical token ownership. If the price of an asset drops 80%, you still own the coins. Nobody can liquidate your position, because you owe nobody borrowed funds. Spot provides a loss limited to the money invested. On a CEX, that asset remains an IOU until withdrawn to a personal wallet, but economically you carry no leverage risk.

## Market orders vs limit orders

Trading on an exchange relies on two fundamental order types:

- **Market order** — demands immediate execution at whatever price the book currently offers. It buys certainty of execution at the cost of certainty of price.
- **Limit order** — executes only at your specified price or better. It buys certainty of price at the cost of certainty of execution. The order rests in the book until another participant crosses the spread to fill it, and it may never execute if the market moves away.

The choice is always an explicit trade-off: certainty of execution versus certainty of price.

## Reading the order book

The order book is the live list of resting orders awaiting execution. It contains two sides:

- **Bids** — resting buy orders, stacked from the highest price downward. The highest buy is the best bid.
- **Asks** — resting sell orders, stacked from the lowest price upward. The lowest sell is the best ask.

The gap between the highest bid and the best ask is the spread. The total volume available across resting orders at each price level defines market depth.

::figure{id=fig-m02-orderbook-depth}

When you submit a market order, the exchange walks the order book, consuming resting orders level by level until the requested size is filled. In a thin book, each filled level pushes the next execution to a worse price. The difference between the price shown when you clicked and the average execution price is slippage.

In the figure above, the lowest available asks are 500 units at 2.00, 800 units at 2.02, and 1,500 units at 2.05. A market buy for 2,000 units cannot fill entirely at 2.00:

- 500 units fill at 2.00 = 1,000 USDT
- 800 units fill at 2.02 = 1,616 USDT
- 700 units fill at 2.05 = 1,435 USDT
- Total: 4,051 USDT for 2,000 units → average fill of 2.026 USDT.

The visible price was 2.00, but the fill average was 2.026—costing approximately 1.3% in slippage before trading fees. A careless market order on an illiquid pair can erase several percent of capital before the trade even begins.

## Account security: keeping the account yours

Exchange accounts face constant credential stuffing, phishing, and SIM-swapping. Protecting an account requires removing single points of failure:

- **Two-factor authentication (2FA).** Use an authenticator app generating time-based codes or a hardware security key. Never rely on SMS verification; phone numbers can be hijacked through mobile carrier social engineering.
- **API key permissions.** When connecting third-party charting tools or tracking software, generate read-only API keys. Never enable withdrawal permissions on an automated key.
- **Withdrawal address whitelisting.** Enable address whitelisting so withdrawals can only be sent to pre-approved addresses, locked behind a mandatory 24-hour delay before new addresses become active.

:::note{type=warning}
Security measures cannot make an exchange impossible to compromise, but they ensure that a leaked password alone cannot drain your account.
:::
