# Behavioural interviews: STAR and story archetypes

## The STAR method and quantifying results
<!-- bullets: 12.3.a -->

:::judgement{source="Will Larson, The STAR method" ref="lethain-star-method#star-method-for-interview-questions"}
According to Will Larson, the STAR format comes down to four parts: the Situation surrounding your task, the Task you undertook or were assigned, the Actions to accomplish the task, and the Results of your actions. He writes that he has never used the STAR method in interviews but has organically arrived at a similar structure. In his experience, one challenge in interviews is that structure can get in the way of seeming engaged and present. He still finds it helpful to practise formatting stories this way with other people.
:::

:::judgement{source="Amazon, SDM interview prep" ref="amazon-sdm-interview-prep#:R354j6H1:"}
According to Amazon's interview preparation page for software development managers, a candidate should recall specific details of memorable experiences from previous jobs. Amazon says that because it is a data-driven company, answers should include metrics or data where applicable. It asks for examples that show how the candidate took risks, succeeded, failed and grew, and for well-structured answers that use the STAR method.
:::

:::judgement{source="Amazon, SDM interview prep" ref="amazon-sdm-interview-prep#:R2t4j6H1:"}
According to the same Amazon page, a significant part of the behavioural interview covers how the candidate demonstrated Amazon's Leadership Principles in past jobs, because past behaviour is an indicator of future success. Amazon says it focuses on the what and how of the experiences and the why of the decisions. Each interviewer typically asks two or three behavioural questions about successes or challenges and how the candidate handled them.
:::

## What interviewers assess at Senior and Staff level
<!-- bullets: 12.3.b -->

:::judgement{source="Will Larson, Staff Engineer: Staff archetypes" ref="staffeng-staff-archetypes#staff-archetypes"}
According to Will Larson, most career ladders define a single uniform set of expectations for Staff engineers, although the Staff-plus roles behind the title cluster into four patterns. He names them the Tech Lead, the Architect, the Solver and the Right Hand. He writes that most companies emphasize one or two of the patterns, and that one pattern exists only in companies with many hundreds or thousands of engineers.
:::

:::judgement{source="Will Larson, Staff Engineer: Staff archetypes" ref="staffeng-staff-archetypes#tech-lead"}
According to Will Larson, the work of a Tech Lead is the most similar to the work of a Senior engineer, which makes it a fairly intuitive first Staff role. He says Tech Leads default to delegating the most complex projects, both to grow teammates and because the team's impact grows as the Tech Lead's coding shrinks. He adds that being a Staff engineer is not just a role but the intersection of the role, behaviours, impact and the organization's recognition of them.
:::

:::judgement{source="GitLab, Engineering career framework: Staff" ref="gitlab-engineering-matrix-staff#engineering"}
According to GitLab's engineering career framework, a staff engineer generally operates at team-level scope and is the technical leader for one or more of the team's domains. The framework lists competencies such as making responsible decisions, evaluating tradeoffs, explaining the merits of solutions to stakeholders and unblocking team members. It also says a staff engineer takes part, as a directly responsible individual, in processes for complex technical challenges such as the architecture design process.
:::

:::judgement{source="GitLab, Engineering career framework: Staff" ref="gitlab-engineering-matrix-staff#engineering-function-competencies-staff"}
According to the same framework, staff engineers at GitLab are expected to show leadership competencies, technical competencies and values alignment, in addition to all the competencies exhibited by a Senior.
:::

## Story archetypes
<!-- bullets: 12.3.c -->

:::judgement{source="Martin Fowler, Strangler Fig" ref="fowler-strangler-fig#banner"}
According to Martin Fowler, replacing a serious IT system takes a long time and users cannot wait for new features, so a plan to build a new system that does exactly what the old one does often fails. He and his colleagues prefer a gradual process that starts with small additions built on top of the legacy code base and moves bits of behaviour into the new code. He says the approach makes investment and returns occur gradually and visibly, and that it does not make the work easy.
:::

:::judgement{source="Andrew Stribblehill, Site Reliability Engineering, ch. 14 Managing Incidents" ref="sre-book-managing-incidents#recursive-separation-of-responsibilities"}
According to Andrew Stribblehill, everybody involved in an incident should know their role and not stray onto someone else's. He writes that a clear separation of responsibilities gives individuals more autonomy because they need not second-guess colleagues. He names four roles that should go to particular people: incident command, operational work, communication and planning.
:::

:::judgement{source="Andrew Stribblehill, Site Reliability Engineering, ch. 14 Managing Incidents" ref="sre-book-managing-incidents#when-to-declare-an-incident"}
According to Andrew Stribblehill, it is better to declare an incident early and find a simple fix than to start the incident framework hours into a growing problem. He asks for clear conditions for declaring one. His team treats an event as an incident if a second team is needed, if customers can see the outage, or if the issue is unsolved after an hour of concentrated analysis.
:::

:::judgement{source="John Lunney and Sue Lueder, Site Reliability Engineering, ch. 15 Postmortem Culture" ref="sre-book-postmortem-culture#googles-postmortem-philosophy"}
According to John Lunney and Sue Lueder, the primary goals of a postmortem are to document the incident, to understand all contributing root causes and to put effective preventive actions in place. They write that a blameless postmortem focuses on the contributing causes without indicting any individual or team, and assumes everyone involved had good intentions and did the right thing with the information they had. In their account, when postmortems move from blame to the systemic reasons someone had incomplete information, effective prevention becomes possible.
:::

:::judgement{source="John Lunney and Sue Lueder, Site Reliability Engineering, ch. 15 Postmortem Culture" ref="sre-book-postmortem-culture#best-practice-avoid-blame-and-keep-it-constructive"}
According to John Lunney and Sue Lueder, removing blame from a postmortem gives people the confidence to escalate issues without fear. They add that an atmosphere of blame risks a culture in which incidents are swept under the rug, which leads to greater risk for the organization.
:::

:::judgement{source="Hyrum Wright, Software Engineering at Google, ch. 15 Deprecation" ref="abseil-swe-ch15#why_is_deprecation_so_hardquestion_mark"}
According to Hyrum Wright, migrating to an entirely new system is extremely expensive at Google, and the costs are frequently underestimated. He says evolving a system in place can be easier, because incremental deprecation through in-place refactoring breaks the work into smaller chunks that yield incremental benefits.
:::

## A template for your own stories
<!-- bullets: 12.3.d -->

:::judgement{source="Will Larson, The STAR method" ref="lethain-star-method#how-to-use"}
According to Will Larson, a person can keep a repository of STAR narratives from a template. Once a year or so, he suggests thinking through recent work that fits a "Tell me about a time when you…" question. For each story the author answers four sections: Situation, Task, Actions and Results. He recommends sharing each answer with a coworker who was there, to find missing pieces, and with someone who was not, to find what is missing for people outside the work. He also recommends rereading the narratives before starting an interview process.
:::
