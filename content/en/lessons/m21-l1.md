# The carry: funding and basis as a strategy

m19-l1, derivatives data, mentioned an arbitrage in passing. The perp trades above spot, so somebody
sells the rich perp and buys the cheap spot, and the two prices get pulled back together. This
lesson studies that sentence from the inside, because the trade it describes is enormous and it is
sold to beginners as free money.

It is not free money. It is a thin-margin business with fat tails.

## Cash-and-carry, laid bare

The trade is one idea with two legs:

- **Buy the spot.** 1 BTC, held on the exchange, at 60,000 USDT.
- **Short the perpetual.** 1 BTC of contracts, at the same 60,000.

You are now delta-neutral. If BTC goes to 70,000 you make 10,000 on the spot and lose 10,000 on the
short; if it goes to 50,000 the two swap places. Your position has no opinion about price at all.

You hold it because, when the perp trades above spot, funding is positive, and positive funding
means longs pay shorts (m04-l1). You are the short. Every eight hours, the crowd of leveraged longs
pays you for the privilege of being long.

**The numbers.** Funding at 0.01% per 8h — a common, unremarkable rate — on a 60,000 USDT short:

- per interval: `60,000 × 0.0001 = 6 USDT`
- per day (three intervals): `18 USDT`
- per year: `18 × 365 = 6,570 USDT`, which on the 120,000 USDT the whole position ties up (60,000 of
  spot plus 60,000 of collateral behind the short) is about 5.5% a year.

That is its shape: a 5%-ish annual yield, delta-neutral, on a large amount of capital, in
exchange for running two positions on an exchange. It is a real return and it is not a lottery
ticket. It is *not* 5% of your account per year unless your whole account is in it. And it is not
free: the capital is locked, and doing nothing with 120,000 USDT would have earned you nothing, but
would also have needed no exchange.

:::note{type=info}
The basis — the perp's premium over spot — and the funding rate are one fact seen twice, which
m19-l1 already said. That means the carry can be entered from either side: you are collecting
funding *because* the perp is rich, and if you also close both legs once the premium compresses, you
capture the basis narrowing as well. Same trade, two ways of describing the profit.
:::

## Why the gap exists at all

If this is a positive expected return with no directional risk, it is hard to see why it is still
there.

Partly it is not: desks run this at size. With them in the trade, the premium on major coins
normally sits well inside a tenth of a percent. The gap that survives is the part the arbitrage
cannot reach, and it survives for ordinary, boring reasons:

- **Capital is not free.** Every dollar in this trade is a dollar not doing something else. That 5%
  has to beat whatever else that money could earn — including, at times, plain lending.
- **The two legs need to sit somewhere.** Spot on one venue and the short on another means
  transfers, withdrawal limits and counterparty risk on both. On a single venue it is simpler and
  the risk is concentrated instead of split. There is no arrangement without a cost.
- **Nobody can arbitrage an infinite amount.** The trade caps out at the collateral available, and
  the people who run it are managing a book, not chasing every basis point.

And the corollary you can use: the gap widens when the crowd is maddest. In a mania, leveraged longs
bid the perp far over spot and funding goes to 0.1% per 8h and beyond — over 100% annualised. That
is when the carry pays spectacularly. The carry's yield measures leverage mania: the same reading
m19-l1 takes from the other side of the table.

## The risks of "riskless"

### 1. Funding flips sign

Positive funding pays you. Negative funding bills you, at the same clock. If the perp swings to a
discount — a crash, a wave of panic shorts (m19-l2) — you are a short paying longs, and the
collection becomes a payment.

Concretely: at −0.05% per 8h your 60,000 short pays out `60,000 × 0.0005 × 3 = 90 USDT a day`. Five
days of that is 450 USDT. A full month of collecting at 0.01% brings in `30 × 18 = 540`, so five
days of paying wipes out five sixths of a month of collecting. You collect small amounts, reliably,
and pay large amounts occasionally: thin margin, fat tail.

### 2. Venue risk

Two legs on two exchanges is two sets of counterparty risk, plus the transfer between them at the
moment you least want a transfer to be slow. Two legs on *one* exchange removes the transfer and
concentrates the counterparty risk. Both are m02's "not your keys" applied to a strategy. Your
delta-neutral position is a claim on an intermediary, and the neutrality is only as good as the
intermediary.

### 3. The short leg gets liquidated in a rally

**This is where m06, liquidation, comes back.**

Your position is delta-neutral as a *whole*. The exchange does not liquidate wholes. It liquidates
positions, against the margin in their own account — and it does not know, or care, that you hold
spot somewhere else.

So: BTC rallies 30%. Your spot leg gains 18,000 USDT, on paper, sitting there. Your short leg is
down 18,000 USDT against whatever collateral you posted for it. If that collateral was 60,000, you
are fine. But if you were efficient with capital and posted 15,000 the short is liquidated long
before the rally ends. The spot never rescues it. **You are left holding the winning leg of a hedge
whose losing leg has been closed at the worst possible price**. That is to say, you are left with a
naked long, in a market that has just gone up 30%, entered by accident.

> **Delta-neutral is not margin-neutral.**

The lesson generalises past this trade: a hedge protects you from the price. It does not protect you
from the *mechanics* of the venue holding it. Everything m06 taught about liquidation — that it is
computed per position, per account, against maintenance margin, without regard to your intentions —
applies here unchanged. The way to survive it is unglamorous. Post far more collateral against the
short than the arithmetic requires, and accept the lower yield that follows.

## The honest reading

Put the three risks next to the return and the strategy's true shape appears:

- The upside is bounded by construction. You collect a small rate. The very best case is that the
  rate is high for a while, and even then you are collecting a percentage, not catching a move.
- The downside is not bounded by the same design. Prices running away cannot hurt a hedged position;
  the open-ended part is the *operational* failures: a liquidated leg, an exchange you cannot
  withdraw from, a funding rate that stays negative.
- And the trade compresses itself. The more capital piles into the carry, the more perps get sold
  against spot, the smaller the premium, the smaller the funding. A crowded carry is a carry paying
  less: m19-l1's premium read from the other side of the table. For the same reason, the advertised
  yields belong to the period *before* everybody arrived.

None of that makes it a bad trade. It makes it a capital-hungry business, with operationally
demanding margins, with a tail risk that has to be actively managed rather than hedged away. Sold as
"risk-free yield", it is the same misdescription as "more trades means more profit" in m23-l1. The
number quoted is real, and it is not the number that decides the outcome.
