# Price structure

Price does not wander at random. It leaves a visible record of where buyers and sellers fought. That
record is the structure, and it is the most direct chart-reading you can do: it is the price itself,
not an indicator derived from it. Reading that record means seeing where price stalled, where it
broke, and whether the breaks meant anything.

## Support and resistance are zones, not lines

A support is a price area where buying has repeatedly stepped in and stopped a fall. A
resistance is an area where selling has repeatedly capped a rise. The word to hold onto is *area*.

Buying reliably reappears at the same place because the place is remembered, and memory turns into
orders. Traders who bought the last bounce and watched it work leave bids to buy it again. Traders
who missed the bounce wait there for a second chance. Traders who bought too high and are underwater
put their break-even exits there. A support is not magic in the number: it is a zone of resting buy
orders that the last few visits taught people to leave there. Resistance is the same thing with sell
orders.

That is also why it is a zone, not a line — a band a few candles wide — because:

- Different traders placed their orders at slightly different prices.
- Wicks and closes tell different stories; the "level" is smeared across both.
- The more times an area is tested, the wider and more real the zone becomes, not the more precise.

Concrete case: say BTC has turned up three times in the same neighbourhood. Once it wicked down to
58,050 and closed back at 58,600, once it based around 58,300 for several candles, once it dipped to
58,150 intraday and recovered. There is no single support *price* in that story. There is a zone
roughly 58,000–58,400 wide. Draw the zone from the bodies and wicks of the swings that turned. Expect
price to react *somewhere inside it*, not at one exact number.

:::note{type=warning}
The most common self-inflicted wound: drawing a level, watching price miss it by 0.3%, and
concluding "support failed." It didn't fail — you drew a line where the market drew a band. A zone
that gets poked and holds is doing its job.
:::

## Breakout vs fakeout

Sooner or later price leaves the range. The question is whether it *meant* it.

- A genuine breakout closes decisively beyond the level — a full candle body on the far side
  — and then holds there. Ideally, it comes back to *retest* the old level as new support (or
  resistance) and bounces away. Extra credit if the break happens on higher volume: it took
  real participation to get through.
- A fakeout (false break) pokes beyond the level — often on a long wick — and then closes
  back inside the range. The break is rejected. These trap the traders who bought the first
  candle past the line.

A genuine breakout tends to retest and hold because of *role reversal*. The sellers who defended the
old resistance have been overrun, and many cover their shorts on the pullback to the level: former
sellers become buyers. Meanwhile the buyers who refused to chase finally get their entry at the old
line. The level that used to cap price now catches it — resistance becomes support.

A fakeout happens because resting orders pile up just beyond the obvious level. They are the
stop-losses of traders positioned into the level and the buy orders of breakout traders, who leave
"buy if it breaks" instructions. Push price a hair past the level and you trigger all of them at
once — a burst of buying with nothing real behind it. Once those orders are spent, price falls back
inside. The ones who bought the first candle through the level are now trapped above the level and
motivated to sell.

Concretely, using the numbers in the figure below: resistance sits around 2,130. The two panels are
the same chart — the same range, the same candles — right up to the decision. On the left, a genuine
break: price closes beyond the line, pushes on to 2,225, and never comes back. On the right, a
fakeout: the same break reaches 2,166, holds half a dozen candles above the line, then loses them. It
closes back under 2,130, gives up 2,035 within a few candles, and keeps sliding from there.

::figure{id=fig-m08-breakout-vs-fakeout}

The single most useful discriminator: **wait for the close, and for that close to hold.** A wick
through a level is not enough, and neither are a few candles closing on the other side: the
right-hand panel has them and still fails. What counts is a body that closes beyond and *stays*.

:::note{type=info}
Nobody can tell a breakout from a fakeout at the instant price crosses the line — not you, not
anyone. That is why patient traders wait for the close beyond, and often for the retest, accepting
they will miss the very first move in exchange for not being trapped by most fakeouts.
:::

## Market structure: HH/HL and LH/LL

Zoom out from the single level to the sequence of swings.

- An uptrend is a sequence of higher highs (HH) and higher lows (HL). Each rally exceeds
  the last peak, and each dip bottoms above the last dip.
- A downtrend is the mirror: lower highs (LH) and lower lows (LL). Each bounce fails
  lower, each drop goes further.

The sequence of highs and lows tells you who is winning without asking any indicator. Higher highs
mean buyers keep finding other buyers willing to pay more; higher lows mean every dip is bought
before it gives back the last gain. As long as that holds, the path of least resistance is up, and
betting against it is betting against the visible balance of force. When the sequence breaks off,
that assumption stops holding.

A clean uptrend might read: low at 100, high at 110, dip to 104 (a higher low), high at 118 (a higher
high), dip to 109 (higher low), high at 126. Each low above the previous low, each high above the
previous high — a sequence you could describe to someone over the phone.

A pullback is the counter-move *within* a trend — the dips to 104 and 109 above. It exists
because not everyone acts at once. Some buyers take profit into strength, some late sellers try their
luck, and price gives back part of the last leg before the trend resumes. It is the trend catching
its breath, not reversing. The catch is that at the moment it forms you cannot tell a pullback from
the start of a reversal — only the next swing settles it. A dip to 109 that turns back up into a new
high *was* a pullback; the same dip that keeps going and breaks 104 was the first crack.

## Change of character

A trend stays intact as long as the sequence of highs and lows continues. It is broken by a change of
character (CHoCH): the first swing that violates the pattern.

In an uptrend, that is the first time price makes a lower low — it fails to hold above the
previous HL. In a downtrend, it is the first higher high. That first break carries so much weight
because the whole uptrend rested on one mechanism — every dip being bought above the previous dip. A
lower low is the first time that mechanism visibly fails: the buyers did not show up where they had
been showing up. In the sequence above, price falling from 126 down through 109 to a low of 106 is a
change of character. It is the first lower low in a run that had made only higher ones.

That break's twin has a name too, though this course does not need it to explain the structure.
Every higher high that takes out the previous high is a break of structure (BOS). It is the break
that *confirms* the pattern, as against the CHoCH, which breaks it. One structure, two kinds of
break. That vocabulary, and the rest of the dialect it comes with, is mapped onto this same mechanic
in m34-l1.

Everything in this lesson draws its levels horizontally, which is where the mechanism is. The same
structure can also be described with *sloped* lines — trendlines and channels. m15-l1 takes that up,
including the account of how much weaker the sloped version's claim is.

The change of character is not a promise that the trend has reversed. It is the first *evidence* that
the old trend's rule has stopped working. Often it precedes a longer transition or a range, not a
clean flip.

:::note{type=warning}
"Change of character" gets sold as a magic reversal signal. It isn't. It's a broken pattern — a
reason to stop assuming the old trend and start watching for what replaces it. One lower low in an
uptrend can be a deep pullback that resumes; treat the CHoCH as a question, not an answer.
:::

## The expensive misread

Two mistakes do most of the damage this topic causes.

**Chasing the first candle past a level.** The break looks obvious and the candle is green and fast.
The fear of missing it drowns out the plan — so you buy the first candle through the level. But that
candle is what a fakeout is made of. You have bought the burst of triggered stops and breakout
orders at the worst possible price, moments before it reverses back inside. Waiting for the close,
and often the retest, costs you the first few points and spares you most of these traps. The first
candle past a level is the single most expensive candle to trade.

**Calling a reversal on one change of character.** A single lower low feels like permission to flip
short and fade the whole prior trend. But one CHoCH is a broken pattern, not a new trend, and strong
trends print the odd lower low inside a deep pullback all the time. Flip your entire bias on the
first one and the trend will run you over on the resumption. Treat the CHoCH as a question: *has
control changed?* Wait for the answer: a lower high after the lower low, a failed attempt to
reclaim, a second confirming break.

:::note{type=warning}
Both mistakes share a root: acting on the *first* sign instead of the *confirmed* one. The market
pays a premium to whoever is willing to be a candle or two late in exchange for being trapped far less
often.
:::

## Crypto is open 24/7 — and that bends these patterns

Traditional markets close; crypto never does, and the always-on order book changes how structure
behaves.

**Weekend and thin-book fakeouts.** With no closing bell, the dead hours run on a thin order book.
They are the weekends, and the stretch when every major timezone is asleep at once. Fewer resting
orders means far less size is needed to shove price through an obvious level. That is why false
breaks cluster in these windows. A "breakout" printed at 4 a.m. on a Sunday deserves more suspicion
than the same break in a busy weekday session. The level may have been pushed through empty air and
will snap back when real volume returns.

**Stop-hunt wicks.** Leverage concentrates stop-loss orders and liquidation prices just beyond the
obvious swing highs and lows. Everyone watches the same levels, so everyone's protective orders end
up in the same narrow band. In a thin book a large player can push price into that band, trip the
clustered stops and force the liquidation of over-leveraged traders. Then it can absorb the
resulting flood of orders and let price snap back. What is left is a long wick that pierced the
level and closed far away from it. (Remember: a *liquidation* here is the exchange force-closing
those over-leveraged positions, not ordinary selling.) That is why, in crypto, the wick through your
level is often longer than in the textbooks. To judge whether a level broke, read the close, never
the extreme.

## Reading a chart in order

1. Mark the obvious zones first — the ones a stranger would circle. If you have to squint, it isn't a
   level.
2. Read the swing sequence: are you in HH/HL, in LH/LL, or in a messy range with neither?
3. At a level, wait for the close. Beyond and holding = breakout; beyond then back inside =
   fakeout. And ask *when* the break happened — a break in dead weekend liquidity is a weaker signal
   than the same break in a busy session.
4. Let the market confirm before you rename a pullback a reversal. The change of character is the
   earliest reliable warning; there is none before it.
