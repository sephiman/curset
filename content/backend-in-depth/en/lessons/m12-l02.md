# Remote work and asynchronous communication

## Clear written communication
<!-- bullets: 12.2.a -->

A written message has to carry its own context. Google's practices say the first line of a change description should be informative enough that readers skimming history do not need to open the change. GitLab asks people to give as much context as possible, because the company is remote-only and spread around the world, and wants replies that give a deadline or say the work is done. 37signals asks every person and team to write down what they worked on and what comes next.

:::judgement{source="Google, eng-practices: Writing good CL descriptions" ref="eng-practices-cl-descriptions#first-line"}
According to Google's engineering practices, the first line of a change description is a short summary of what the change does, followed by a blank line. By tradition it is a complete sentence written as an order, such as "Delete the FizzBuzz RPC and replace it with the new system." The practices say it should be informative enough that readers skimming history do not need to open the change.
:::

:::judgement{source="Google, eng-practices: Writing good CL descriptions" ref="eng-practices-cl-descriptions#informative"}
According to the same page, the rest of the description fills in the details a reader needs to understand the change as a whole. It says to include enough context for reviewers and future readers, and that even small changes deserve a little attention to detail.
:::

:::judgement{source="GitLab Handbook, Communication" ref="gitlab-communication#top-tips-and-best-practices"}
According to the GitLab handbook, an answer to a request should be either a deadline or a note that the work is done, because replies such as "will do" or "it is on my todo list" are not helpful. It asks for explicit needs together with an @ mention of who must act, since a CC alone may lead to no action. It also asks people to use low-context communication and to give as much context as possible, because the company is remote-only and spread around the world.
:::

:::judgement{source="37signals Handbook, How we work" ref="basecamp-how-we-work#communication"}
According to the 37signals handbook, because the company works asynchronously and remotely it is important to radiate information about what people are working on. It lists four mechanisms: an afternoon question about what each person worked on that day, which people must answer at least twice a week, a Monday question about the week ahead, a team kickoff for the upcoming cycle, and a team heartbeat that summarizes the work completed in the previous cycle. It says clear expectations for communication make it easier for everyone to build trust in where the company is going and why.
:::

## Asynchronous unblocking
<!-- bullets: 12.2.a -->

Both handbooks make a written request the first move and keep synchronous contact for the cases that need it. 37signals has people post a request that others read on their own schedule, and GitLab has them ask in issues or public channels where many can answer. The Google book names not asking for help when stuck as one of the biggest mistakes beginners make, and says that when information must be shared outside a group chat or kept for later reference, a person should write a document or email a mailing list.

:::judgement{source="GitLab Handbook, Asynchronous communication" ref="gitlab-communication#asynchronous-communication"}
According to the GitLab handbook, asynchronous communication is communicating and moving projects forward without needing other stakeholders to be available at the same time the message is sent. The handbook describes Slack, documents and issues as its asynchronous layer, which scales across time zones and leaves durable artifacts. It says some decisions still need a synchronous exchange, such as aligning on a strategy shift or resolving a real disagreement. In its account, an async-only habit leaves threads that fork, context that drifts and no clear record of who decided what.
:::

:::judgement{source="GitLab Handbook, Communication" ref="gitlab-communication#top-tips-and-best-practices"}
According to the GitLab handbook, people should use asynchronous communication when possible, with merge requests preferred and issues next. It says nobody is expected to be available all the time or to respond outside their planned working hours. Synchronous communication is sometimes better, such as a video call when someone is blocked, but the handbook says not to default to it. It asks people to ask questions where many can answer and benefit, in issues or public channels rather than direct messages.
:::

:::judgement{source="GitLab Handbook, Communication" ref="gitlab-communication#start-with-a-merge-request"}
According to the GitLab handbook, a discussion is best started with a merge request instead of an issue, because a merge request is tied to a specific proposed change and is actionable. It says to submit the smallest viable and valuable change and to cross-link related issues and merge requests. It also says that when a change is accepted or rejected, the description should be updated with the final conclusion.
:::

:::judgement{source="37signals Handbook, How we work" ref="basecamp-how-we-work#asynchronously"}
According to the 37signals handbook, most work should not require constant communication with someone throughout the day. It says people should collaborate as though most requests will get an answer eventually but not necessarily right now, and that their first choice should be to post a message, a todo or a document about what they need to explain or know, so that others read it on their own schedule instead of being interrupted in their peak flow time. Pings, video calls, screen-sharing or in-person collaboration are for when async is not efficient, and the handbook says those cases should be infrequent.
:::

:::judgement{source="Nina Chen and Mark Barolak, Software Engineering at Google, ch. 3 Knowledge Sharing" ref="abseil-swe-ch3#ask_questions"}
According to Nina Chen and Mark Barolak, one of the biggest mistakes beginners make is not to ask for help when they are stuck. They write that people in leadership roles should model asking questions, since openly asking or expressing gaps in knowledge reinforces that it is OK for others to do the same. On the receiving end, they say patience and kindness when answering make people feel safe looking for help.
:::

:::judgement{source="Nina Chen and Mark Barolak, Software Engineering at Google, ch. 3 Knowledge Sharing" ref="abseil-swe-ch3#group_chats"}
According to Nina Chen and Mark Barolak, a group chat lets a person ask many people at once and have a quick back-and-forth with whoever is available, and many forms of group chat can be archived and searched later. They say group chats provide little structure, so as soon as information must be shared outside the group or kept for later reference, a person should write a document or email a mailing list.
:::

## Living documentation
<!-- bullets: 12.2.a -->

Manshreck treats documentation like code: it sits under source control, is reviewed with the code it describes and has an owner. He says documents without owners become stale, and that old documents can cause problems as old code does.

:::judgement{source="Tom Manshreck, Software Engineering at Google, ch. 10 Documentation" ref="abseil-swe-ch10#documentation_is_like_code"}
According to Tom Manshreck, documentation is so tightly coupled to code that it should be treated as code, placed under source control and reviewed along with the code it documents. He says documents should have clear owners, because documents without owners become stale and difficult to maintain. When two documents conflict, he recommends designating a canonical document and consolidating the others into it or deprecating the duplicates. He also lists tracking issues against documents like bugs and periodically evaluating them.
:::

:::judgement{source="Tom Manshreck, Software Engineering at Google, ch. 10 Documentation" ref="abseil-swe-ch10#deprecating_documents"}
According to Tom Manshreck, old documents can cause problems as old code does. When a document no longer serves a purpose, he says to remove it or mark it obsolete and point to where the new information lives. He describes Google attaching freshness dates to documents, with email reminders when a document has not been touched for a period such as three months. He reports that adding a "Last reviewed by" byline with the owner increased adoption.
:::

## Time-zone overlap
<!-- bullets: 12.2.a -->

Google's practices want a reviewer in another time zone to answer while the author still has working day left to respond. GitLab names working-hour windows for meetings across regions and suggests UTC for recurring meetings. Its handbook also says meetings should have notes, to enable asynchronous participation.

:::judgement{source="Google, eng-practices: Speed of Code Reviews" ref="eng-practices-speed#tz"}
According to Google's engineering practices, a reviewer working across time zones should try to answer the author while the author still has time to respond before the end of their working day. If the author has already finished for the day, the practices say the review should be done before they start the next day.
:::

:::judgement{source="Google, eng-practices: Speed of Code Reviews" ref="eng-practices-speed#responses"}
According to the same page, response time matters more than the time a change takes to get through the whole review. A reviewer who is too busy for a full review can still reply with when they will get to it, suggest other reviewers or give initial broad comments. The practices say this should be sent at a reasonable break point and not by interrupting focused work.
:::

:::judgement{source="Google, eng-practices: Speed of Code Reviews" ref="eng-practices-speed#why"}
According to the same page, slow reviews lower the velocity of the team, make developers protest the review process and put code health at risk. The practices say most complaints about code review are actually resolved by making it faster.
:::

:::judgement{source="GitLab Handbook, Communication" ref="gitlab-communication#cross-regional-working-hours-recommendations"}
According to the GitLab handbook, a meeting across several regions can consider using time ranges that respect common working hours, with each suggested window shown in local time. For a meeting with team members in EMEA and AMER its example is 8:00 AM to 10:00 AM Pacific Time. It notes that time zone offsets change through the year with daylight saving and similar changes, so the suggested times may be less convenient at different times of year.
:::

:::judgement{source="GitLab Handbook, Communication" ref="gitlab-communication#scheduling-meetings"}
According to the GitLab handbook, for team members in AMER time zones who regularly collaborate with EMEA colleagues, the PST morning block should be reserved for cross-regional collaboration, and a meeting whose attendees are all in AMER time zones should be scheduled outside it. It suggests using Coordinated Universal Time as the time zone of recurring meetings, so that the meeting time does not change for others when a person's local time zone changes.
:::

:::judgement{source="GitLab Handbook, Communication" ref="gitlab-communication#everyone-is-responsible-for-notes"}
According to the GitLab handbook, meetings should have notes, both as a single source of truth and to enable async participation. It says written notes are more efficient to read than meeting recordings and offer greater opportunities for async engagement, and that notes should be taken even when a meeting is recorded.
:::

## Key points

- According to the GitLab handbook, a reply to a request should be a deadline or a note that the work is done, and a request should name who must act with an @ mention.
- According to the GitLab handbook, people should use asynchronous communication when possible, with merge requests preferred and issues next, and keep synchronous communication for cases such as a blocked colleague.
- According to the 37signals handbook, the first choice for a request is to post a message, a todo or a document that others can read on their own schedule.
- According to Tom Manshreck, documents should have clear owners, and a document that no longer serves a purpose should be removed or marked obsolete.
- According to Google's engineering practices, response time matters more than the time a change takes to get through the whole review.
- According to the GitLab handbook, recurring meetings can use UTC so that their time does not change for others when a local time zone changes.
