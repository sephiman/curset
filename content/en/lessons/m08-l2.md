# The reaction: reading candles at a level

Over and over this course tells you to "wait for price to react at the level". It says so at a
broken support on the retest (m08), at a Fibonacci pullback (m13 step 4) and at the spring that
tests a Wyckoff range (m09). This lesson says what a *reaction* looks like.

The trick is to stop thinking of candles as a catalogue of shapes to memorise. Read them as the
footprint of order flow — a compact record of who tried what in that period, and who won. Almost
everything you need reduces to two mechanics: a rejection (price was pushed to a price and thrown
back) and an engulfing (one side steamrolled the other). Plus a third category that matters just as
much: candles that mean nothing, and knowing not to trade them.

## Rejection: the wick that lost a battle

**What it is.** A rejection is a long wick with a small body — the classic *hammer* (long lower wick) or *shooting star* (long upper wick). The body is where price opened and closed; the wick is how far it travelled and was forced back.

**Why it matters.** A long wick is the record of a lost battle. Price was pushed to a level, met
enough opposing orders to absorb the push, and returned. The side that drove price into the wick
*tried and failed*; the side that absorbed it *won that battle*. That is real information about
order flow — but only about the battle at the wick's tip.

**In practice.** Price falls to a support at 61,000, wicks down to 60,400, then closes back at
61,200. That is a ~600-point lower wick under a tiny body. Sellers pushed price ~1% below support;
buyers absorbed the whole push and drove it back above. The wick records sellers losing the battle
*at 61,000* — a price that, as we'll see, has to matter for the wick to mean anything.

## Engulfing: the body that steamrolled

**What it is.** An *engulfing* candle: a large body whose range fully covers the prior candle's body — the previous bar's whole story invalidated in one period.

**Why it matters.** Where a rejection is a battle *won at an extreme*, an engulfing is one side
steamrolling the other. The prior candle said one thing; the engulfing candle erases it. When that
happens *at a level*, it says the side defending the level just got run over.

**In practice.** The prior candle is red, closing down 0.8%. The next candle opens there and closes
up 1.9%, its body swallowing the previous bar's entire range — a bullish engulfing. Buyers overran
everything the last period's sellers built.

## Non-information: when a candle says nothing

Half of reading candles is refusing to read the ones that carry no message.

**The doji.** Open and close are almost equal — a tiny body, wicks on both sides. Nobody won: the
battle between buyers and sellers ended in a draw. A doji is *indecision*, not a signal. Open
50,000, close 50,050 is a 0.1% body — that candle is the market shrugging.

**The small-range candle.** A very short bar after movement is compression — the market pausing,
coiling. It predicts no direction; it only tells you volatility dropped. Reading a continuation into
it is inventing intent the bar doesn't have. This same phenomenon exists at two larger scales, and
says as little at each. Across a run of bars it is the wedge and the triangle of m15-l2. Across a
whole market regime it is the volatility cycle of m16-l1.

## Location is 90% of the signal

**A candle's meaning comes overwhelmingly from where it happens.**

A rejection wick at the 0.618 retracement, over an old support that has already held twice, is
information. The *level* is what buyers were defending, and the wick shows them defending it. The
identical wick away from any level is noise with a wick. Same shape, completely different value —
because the level, not the candle, does the work. This is m13's stance that "the fib doesn't create
the level": the candle doesn't create the signal either.

::figure{id=fig-m08-rejection-vs-open-space}

So the read is always two questions, in order: *what is the mechanic* (rejection, engulfing, or
nothing), and — the one that decides its weight — *where did it happen*.

## The names you'll hear

You will meet named Japanese candlestick patterns everywhere: YouTube, TradingView, trading Twitter. You should be able to parse that lingua franca, so here is the dictionary — mapped onto the two mechanics plus location, never as standalone signals:

- **Hammer / pin bar** — a *rejection* at lows (long lower wick).
- **Shooting star** — the same *rejection* inverted at highs (long upper wick).
- **Engulfing** — the *engulfing* mechanic itself (one body swallows the prior range).
- **Morning star / evening star** — a *rejection* told across three candles instead of one (down,
  pause, up — or the mirror).
- **Harami** — *compression* right after an impulse (a small candle inside the prior body).
- **Tweezers** — a *double rejection* at the same level (two candles wicking to the same price).

::figure{id=fig-m08-multi-candle-forms}

There aren't 60 patterns, there are two mechanics wearing 60 names. And like m13's self-fulfilling
levels, a widely watched name matters *a little* more because thousands of traders are reading it
the same way and acting on it. That is also its limit: the edge is thin because it is crowded, and
never a substitute for the level underneath. Whenever this course uses a name, it's shorthand for
"this mechanic, at this location".

## Reading more than the candle says

All three ways this knowledge backfires are the same error: reading the form and forgetting the
location.

The first is spotting a textbook hammer or engulfing *anywhere* and taking it. A rejection that
rejected nothing, an engulfing that overran nothing anyone was defending — these fire constantly and
mean almost nothing on their own. If you can't name the level the candle is reacting to, there is no
trade.

The second is collecting patterns like Pokémon, memorising sixty names as sixty separate buy/sell
buttons. It feels like knowledge and performs like noise, because it skips the only question that
matters. Learn the two mechanics; treat every name as a pointer to one of them at a location.

The third is reading intent into a doji. A doji at a random spot is indecision. Traders talk
themselves into "reversal doji" after the fact. Live, it is the market telling you it hasn't decided
— which is not permission to decide for it.

## Candle reactions in crypto

Crypto sharpens both the signal and the ways it lies. Markets run 24/7, so a great many candles form
in thin weekend and overnight books. There a *handful* of orders can print a dramatic wick that
looks like a heavyweight rejection and is really a liquidity vacuum. A hammer on a quiet Sunday at a
low-liquidity level is a much weaker tell than the same hammer at a defended level during active
hours. And leverage stacks stops and liquidations just beyond obvious levels. So a long wick through
a level is often a stop-hunt — real order flow, but manufactured, not organic conviction. Weight
every reaction by the depth of the book it happened in and the level it happened at, and distrust a
lonely, spectacular wick in a quiet market.

:::note{type=info}
Read a candle in two moves: name the mechanic (rejection, engulfing, or non-information), then ask where it happened. A reaction at a level the chart already respects is information; the same form away from any level is noise; a doji or small-range candle is no signal at all. The level does the work — the candle just tells you what happened there.
