# Cultural fit and product alignment

## Incident culture and technical maturity
<!-- bullets: 12.4.b -->

:::judgement{source="John Lunney and Sue Lueder, Site Reliability Engineering, ch. 15 Postmortem Culture" ref="sre-book-postmortem-culture#googles-postmortem-philosophy"}
According to John Lunney and Sue Lueder, postmortems are expected after any significant undesirable event, and the criteria should be defined before an incident so that everyone knows when one is needed. Their common triggers are user-visible downtime beyond a threshold, data loss of any kind, on-call engineer intervention, a resolution time above a threshold and a monitoring failure. They state that blameless postmortems are a tenet of SRE culture and that writing a postmortem is not punishment but a learning opportunity for the whole company.
:::

:::judgement{source="Andrew Stribblehill, Site Reliability Engineering, ch. 14 Managing Incidents" ref="sre-book-managing-incidents#best-practices-for-incident-management"}
According to Andrew Stribblehill, good incident management starts with preparation: the procedures are developed and documented in advance, in consultation with the participants. He lists giving full autonomy within the assigned role to all participants, and using the process routinely so that it becomes second nature. He also suggests rotating roles so that every team member becomes familiar with each one.
:::

## On-call load and toil
<!-- bullets: 12.4.b -->

:::judgement{source="Vivek Rau, Site Reliability Engineering, ch. 5 Eliminating Toil" ref="sre-book-eliminating-toil#toil-defined"}
According to Vivek Rau, toil is the kind of work tied to running a production service that tends to be manual, repetitive, automatable, tactical, devoid of enduring value and that scales linearly as a service grows. He says it is not simply work a person dislikes, and that overhead such as team meetings and HR paperwork is not toil. In his account, handling pager alerts is toil because it is interrupt-driven and reactive rather than strategy-driven and proactive.
:::

:::judgement{source="Vivek Rau, Site Reliability Engineering, ch. 5 Eliminating Toil" ref="sre-book-eliminating-toil#why-less-toil-is-better"}
According to Vivek Rau, Google's SRE organization has an advertised goal of keeping operational work below 50% of each SRE's time, with at least half spent on engineering project work. He says the goal exists because toil tends to expand if left unchecked and can fill everyone's time. He adds that the organization must not let itself or any subteam devolve into an Ops team.
:::

:::judgement{source="Vivek Rau, Site Reliability Engineering, ch. 5 Eliminating Toil" ref="sre-book-eliminating-toil#calculating-toil"}
According to Vivek Rau, an SRE on call has a floor of toil. With one week of primary and one week of secondary on-call per cycle, a 6-person rotation puts at least 2 of every 6 weeks on on-call shifts and interrupt handling, a lower bound of 33% of an SRE's time. In an 8-person rotation the lower bound is 25%. He reports that SREs name interrupts as their top source of toil, followed by urgent on-call response and then releases and pushes, and that excessive individual toil often shows managers need to spread it more evenly.
:::

## Autonomy and the relationship with product teams
<!-- bullets: 12.4.b -->

:::judgement{source="Michael Wildpaner and others, The Site Reliability Workbook, ch. 18 SRE Engagement Model" ref="sre-workbook-engagement-model#sre-engagement-model"}
According to the authors of the SRE Workbook chapter on the engagement model, SRE principles aim to maximize the engineering velocity of developer teams while keeping products reliable. They write that even the best SRE team can accomplish only so much, and that with a large production landscape a team must decide where to focus. In their view, product development and SRE teams can collaborate to identify that focus, and the engagement is about understanding the aims of developer and product teams as well as the services.
:::

:::judgement{source="Michael Wildpaner and others, The Site Reliability Workbook, ch. 18 SRE Engagement Model" ref="sre-workbook-engagement-model#aligning-goals"}
According to the same chapter, developer and SRE teams both care about reliability, availability, performance, scalability, efficiency and velocity, but SRE favours long-term viability over new feature launches. The authors suggest that each team keep its own focus while explicitly supporting the goals of the other. They give the example of SRE promising to support releasing as quickly as is safe, where safe generally means staying within the error budget. Developers in turn commit a reasonable share of engineering time to fixing and preventing reliability problems, including paying down technical debt.
:::

## Career expectations
<!-- bullets: 12.4.b -->

:::judgement{source="GitLab, Engineering career framework: Staff" ref="gitlab-engineering-matrix-staff#engineering~2"}
According to GitLab's engineering career framework, a staff engineer has a broad skill set with in-depth expertise in the team's technical domain and looks beyond immediate needs to the longer term. It also says the role includes representing the team as a domain expert to other teams and decomposing complex requirements into a proposal of small deliverables.
:::
