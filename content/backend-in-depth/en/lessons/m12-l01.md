# Architecture documentation, decision making and governance

## Architecture decision records and design documents
<!-- bullets: 12.1.a -->

:::judgement{source="Michael Nygard, Documenting Architecture Decisions" ref="nygard-adr#context"}
According to Michael Nygard, agile methods are not opposed to documentation, only to valueless documentation. He writes that documents that help the team have value only if they are kept up to date, and that large documents are never kept up to date. Small, modular documents have at least a chance of being updated. In his account, the motivation behind a decision is one of the hardest things to track, and a newcomer without it can only blindly accept the decision or blindly change it.
:::

:::judgement{source="Michael Nygard, Documenting Architecture Decisions" ref="nygard-adr#decision"}
According to Michael Nygard, an architecture decision record is a short text file that describes a set of forces and a single decision in response to them. He keeps records for architecturally significant decisions, which he defines as those that affect structure, non-functional characteristics, dependencies, interfaces or construction techniques. His format has a title, a context, a decision, a status and consequences. The context describes the forces at play in value-neutral language. The decision is written in full sentences with active voice, as "We will …". The status is proposed or accepted, and a later record can mark it deprecated or superseded. Records are numbered sequentially, the numbers are never reused, and a reversed decision stays in the repository marked as superseded. He wants each record to be one or two pages long and written as a conversation with a future developer.
:::

:::judgement{source="Michael Nygard, Documenting Architecture Decisions" ref="nygard-adr#decision"}
According to Michael Nygard, the consequences section lists all consequences of the decision, positive, negative and neutral, because all of them affect the team and project in the future.
:::

:::judgement{source="Michael Nygard, Documenting Architecture Decisions" ref="nygard-adr#consequences"}
According to Michael Nygard, the consequences of one record are very likely to become the context of later records. In his view, keeping the records visible lets everyone see the motivation behind previous decisions as the team changes, and shows when it is time to change an old decision.
:::

:::judgement{source="Google, eng-practices: Writing good CL descriptions" ref="eng-practices-cl-descriptions#writing-good-cl-descriptions"}
According to Google's engineering practices, a change description is a public record of the change and it should say what is being changed and why. The practices ask the author to include decisions made that are not reflected in the source code. They note that reading code may reveal what the software does but not why it exists, which makes it harder for future developers to know whether they can move Chesterton's fence.
:::

:::judgement{source="Google, eng-practices: Writing good CL descriptions" ref="eng-practices-cl-descriptions#informative"}
According to the same Google page, the body of a description can include a brief description of the problem being solved and why the chosen approach is the best one. It adds that any shortcomings of the approach should be mentioned, along with background such as bug numbers, benchmark results and links to design documents. It warns that links to external resources may not be visible to future readers because of access restrictions or retention policies.
:::

## Request-for-comments processes
<!-- bullets: 12.1.a -->

:::judgement{source="The Rust project, rust-lang/rfcs README" ref="rust-rfcs-readme#top"}
According to the README of the Rust project's RFC repository, the "RFC" (request for comments) process provides a consistent and controlled path for changes to Rust, such as new features, so that all stakeholders can be confident about the direction of the project. Many changes can go through the normal GitHub pull request workflow, while "substantial" changes are put through a design process and need consensus among the Rust community and the sub-teams. To get a major feature added, the author first gets the RFC merged into the RFC repository as a markdown file, and the RFC then becomes "active" and may be implemented. A sub-team member proposes a motion for final comment period with a disposition of merge, close or postpone, and that period lasts ten calendar days so that it is open for at least five business days. The README says being "active" is not a rubber stamp, and that once accepted RFCs should not be substantially changed: more substantial changes should be new RFCs, with a note added to the original.
:::

## Building consensus and influencing without authority
<!-- bullets: 12.1.b -->

:::judgement{source="Google, eng-practices: The Standard of Code Review" ref="eng-practices-standard#conflicts"}
According to Google's engineering practices, the first step in any conflict on a code review is for the developer and the reviewer to try to reach consensus, based on the written guides. When that is especially difficult, the practices say a face-to-face meeting or video conference can help, and the result should be recorded as a comment for future readers. If that does not settle it, the most common way out is to escalate, often to a broader team discussion, a Technical Lead, a maintainer of the code or an Eng Manager. The practices add that a change should not sit around because the two sides cannot agree.
:::

:::judgement{source="Google, eng-practices: Handling pushback in code reviews" ref="eng-practices-pushback#who_is_right"}
According to Google's engineering practices on pushback, a reviewer whose suggestion is contested should first consider whether the developer is correct, since developers are often closer to the code. If they are, the reviewer says so and lets the issue drop. If they are not, the reviewer explains why the suggestion is right, in a way that shows an understanding of the reply. The practices say improving code health tends to happen in small steps, that it can take a few rounds of explanation, and that the reviewer should stay polite and let the developer know they are heard.
:::

:::judgement{source="Google, eng-practices: Handling pushback in code reviews" ref="eng-practices-pushback#upsetting_developers"}
According to the same page, reviewers sometimes fear that a developer will be upset by an insistence on improvement. The practices say such upset is usually brief, that developers are often thankful later, and that when comments are polite the worry is usually only in the reviewer's mind. In their words, upsets are usually more about how comments are written than about the insistence on code quality.
:::

## Governing the lifecycle when teams generate code with AI
<!-- bullets: 12.1.c -->

:::judgement{source="DORA, Artificial Intelligence research" ref="dora-research-ai#roi-of-ai-assisted-software-development"}
According to DORA, its report on the return on investment of AI-assisted software development provides a practical framework for the complexities of AI adoption. DORA says the guide supplies calculations and conversation starters for a team that is managing the initial productivity dip of a new rollout, and for one that has to defend its budget for the next fiscal year.
:::

:::judgement{source="DORA, Artificial Intelligence research" ref="dora-research-ai#generative-ai-insights"}
According to DORA, its 2024 report includes deep dives into the adoption, attitudes and downstream impacts of AI. DORA lists insights on balancing AI tensions as teams move from AI adoption to effective use across the software development lifecycle, on concerns beyond the accuracy of AI output, and on choosing measurement frameworks to fit organizational goals.
:::

:::judgement{source="DORA, Software delivery performance metrics" ref="dora-metrics#key-insights"}
According to DORA's guide to its delivery metrics, its research has repeatedly shown that speed and stability are not tradeoffs, and that the metrics are correlated for most teams. DORA says the metrics are best suited to one application or service at a time, and that blending them across teams or a whole organization can be problematic because the context differs.
:::

:::judgement{source="DORA, Software delivery performance metrics" ref="dora-metrics#common-pitfalls"}
According to the same DORA guide, setting a metric as a goal ignores Goodhart's law and makes it more likely that teams will game it. It also lists having one metric to rule them all, comparing very different applications, siloed ownership of the metrics and competing against other teams as pitfalls. DORA recommends choosing several metrics, including some with a healthy amount of tension between them.
:::

:::judgement{source="DORA, Capabilities: Trunk-based development" ref="dora-capabilities-trunk#searchLinkMobile"}
According to DORA, in trunk-based development each developer divides work into small batches and merges into trunk at least once a day, and branches typically last no more than a few hours. DORA says this reduces the complexity of merging and keeps code current. It contrasts this with long-lived branches, which need bigger merges and stabilizing efforts such as code freezes.
:::
