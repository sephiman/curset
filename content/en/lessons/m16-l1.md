# Volatility cycles: compression, expansion and the squeeze

m08-l2 gave you a single compression candle and m15-l2 gave you a compression *pattern*. This
lesson takes the same idea one level up, to the whole market: volatility clusters. Quiet stretches
follow quiet stretches, violent ones follow violent ones, and the two take turns.

That is not a piece of folklore — it is one of the most robust statistical regularities in any
traded market. And it explains why "it has been calm for three weeks" is information while "it went
up for three weeks" mostly is not. The useful form of it is short: **a quiet market is not a safe
market. It is a market that has not moved yet.**

## Measuring it: Bollinger

A Bollinger band is the simplest possible measurement of "how dispersed has price been":

- a moving average of the close — the basis;
- plus and minus a number of standard deviations of the close (conventionally two) over the same
  window.

Two standard deviations of the closes, drawn as an envelope. When closes have been scattered, the
band is wide. When they have been bunched, it is narrow. The width is a read of realized volatility
— of what already happened.

This indicator attracts more mythology than most. A touch of the upper band is not a sell signal. It
means price is two standard deviations above its own recent mean. In a trending market, that is
where price lives. Reading it as a sell is the same error m11 named for an overbought RSI, wearing a
different envelope.

## Measuring it: Keltner

A Keltner channel looks identical and is measured with a different ruler:

- the same kind of moving average in the middle;
- plus and minus a multiple of the ATR — the *average true range*, which is how far a typical bar
  travels from high to low, counting any gap between bars.

So the two envelopes answer two different questions:

| | what it measures |
|---|---|
| Bollinger | how far the closes scatter |
| Keltner | how far a bar travels |

Those come apart. Picture a market where every bar swings a long way and closes back near where it
opened. It is busy inside each candle and goes nowhere across them. The ATR is high, so the Keltner
channel is wide; the closes are all in a heap, so the Bollinger band is narrow. Now picture a steady
trend of small, orderly bars: the ATR is low and the closes are strung out all the way up the move,
so it is the other way round.

## The squeeze

> **When the Bollinger band sits entirely inside the Keltner channel, the market is in a squeeze.**

The closes have contracted *relative to how far the bars are still travelling* — an unusual amount
of compression, unusual in the specific sense that the two rulers disagree. Ordinary quiet does not
do it, because ordinary quiet shrinks both. The squeeze ends when the Bollinger band pushes back
outside, and that exit is the expansion.

The figure shows one cycle with both envelopes drawn. The window opens busy, at around 2,014, with
the Bollinger band outside the Keltner channel. Then the closes pin themselves together while the
bars keep moving, and the Bollinger band slides inside: the squeeze. Price sits near 1,902 going
nowhere. When the band pushes back out, the expansion carries it to 1,589.

### The squeeze momentum indicator

The packaged version you will meet under names like *Squeeze Momentum* puts the two ideas in one
pane. A row of dots on a zero line shows whether the squeeze is on, and a signed histogram shows the
direction and force of the move.

The figure below adds that pane, on a second instance so you can see the reading hold twice. The window
opens near 10,361, compresses around 10,551, and the expansion carries it to 12,272.

::figure{id=fig-m16-squeeze-momentum}

The pane adds nothing the price panel above it does not already contain. The dots are the
Bollinger-inside-Keltner condition and the histogram is a smoothed measure of where price sits
relative to its own recent range. It is a convenience — one pane instead of four lines — and it
repackages two averages: it knows nothing more than they do.

## The word "squeeze" means two unrelated things

**The collision is total, and the two mechanisms have nothing to do with each other.**

1. Volatility squeeze (this lesson): a *measurement artefact* — the Bollinger band inside the
   Keltner channel. It describes how compressed price has been. There is no forced participant and
   no liquidation.
2. Short squeeze (m19-l2): a *market event*. Price rises into a cluster of short liquidation prices,
   and those shorts are forcibly bought back. That buying pushes price higher, which liquidates more
   shorts. A feedback loop with real forced orders in it.

One is a statement about a chart. The other is a statement about who is being forced to trade and in
which direction. They share a word and nothing else. If somebody says "there's a squeeze setting
up", in one of the two senses nothing is happening.

## What compression actually tells you

The closing reading, and it is the same shape as m19's open interest and funding:

> **Compression says an expansion is coming. It does not say which way.**

A squeeze is a statement about the *size* of the next move, not its direction. The same goes for
rising open interest, which tells you positions are being added without saying by whom. And for
funding, which tells you which side is paying without saying which side is right. The tool describes
a condition; the direction you read from the break, with the same body-close and participation
discipline m08-l1 and m14 ask for everywhere else.

A failure mode follows directly from that. The expansion out of a squeeze is often violent, which
means a trade taken on the wrong side of it loses quickly. "The squeeze is about to fire" is not a
direction. A position sized as though it were is sized on nothing — which is m22, risk management,
and m26, the psychology of mistakes, again.
