# What crypto actually is

Before opening a trade, you need a clear picture of what you are trading. A trader deposits funds, sees a balance on a screen, and assumes it works like a bank account. It does not. Send coins to the wrong address or choose the wrong network, and the transfer is gone forever. No support desk can reverse it. Crypto is not magic internet money and it is not a scam by definition. It is software running on a shared ledger. You trade its price swings, but you survive by knowing what supports that price and where the trapdoors are.

## A blockchain, at user level

A blockchain is a shared ledger: a list of transactions that independent computers ("nodes") keep and agree on. Transactions are bundled into blocks, and each block points cryptographically to the previous one. The chain forms a tamper-evident history where altering an older record breaks every block stacked after it.

Trading does not require understanding the cryptography, but it does require understanding three operational rules:

- **No central operator.** No company's server is Bitcoin. Thousands of independent nodes hold the same history and reject any transaction that violates the protocol. No single party can print extra coins, freeze your balance, or reverse a transfer. Changing a core rule requires consensus across the entire network.
- **Settlement is final.** Once a transaction confirms on-chain, reversing it is impossible. Undoing it would require out-computing the rest of the honest network to rewrite every subsequent block. If you send tokens to an incorrect address or to a scammer, there is no chargeback. The finality that protects you from censorship also removes your safety net.
- **You hold keys, not accounts.** The blockchain has no concept of your identity or email address. Ownership is proven exclusively by a private key. Whoever controls that secret number controls the coins. Lose the key and your funds remain locked on-chain forever. Leak the key and your balance is stolen instantly. That is the reality of self-custody.

:::note{type=warning}
"Not your keys, not your coins." If your coins sit on an exchange, you own an IOU from that company, not the coins on-chain. When an exchange halts withdrawals or goes bankrupt, your balance becomes an unsecured credit claim.
:::

## Bitcoin vs altcoins vs stablecoins

These three categories behave differently, and treating them alike is how beginners lose capital.

Bitcoin (BTC) is the oldest and most decentralised network, designed with a fixed issuance schedule capped at 21 million coins. It functions as the benchmark asset of the crypto market because it has the longest track record, the widest distribution, and no central foundation. Traders quote and measure market risk against BTC. When alts bleed against Bitcoin, BTC holds value while other assets fall relative to it.

Altcoins are everything other than Bitcoin. Anyone can deploy a token in an afternoon with minimal capital. The quality range spans from serious settlement platforms with heavy developer activity down to memecoins launched to extract liquidity from retail buyers. The label tells you nothing about durability.

Stablecoins (USDT, USDC) are tokens designed to track a fixed value, usually 1 USD. They serve as the cash leg of crypto trading, allowing you to park value between trades without touching a bank. On futures venues they are usually also the collateral that margins your open trades. A stablecoin is a credit exposure to its issuer and reserves. When an algorithmic peg breaks or banking reserves freeze, collateral across every open position distorts at once.

## What confers durable value

Durable value comes from three factors: demand for real use, credible scarcity, and network effects. Real use means participants need the network to settle value, trade, or run applications. Credible scarcity means supply limits cannot be rewritten by a developer team at will. Network effects mean liquidity and infrastructure compound, making the network harder to displace over time.

A polished website, a celebrity sponsorship, or a paid partnership does not confer value. Neither does an aggressive narrative or a price that doubled last week. A narrative can drive price for weeks and still collapse to zero over months.

## Two myths with a price tag

Two mistakes repeatedly cost beginners money:

**Mistaking market cap for real money.** Market cap equals the current token price multiplied by total supply. That quoted price is set by the last trade, even if that trade moved only fifty dollars. Suppose a project creates 1 billion tokens, but only 1%—10 million tokens—circulates in the market. At a price of 2 USD, the headline market cap reads 2 billion USD. If genuine bids in the order book total only 200,000 USD, selling a modest position crashes the price. A large market cap on a thin book is an illusion that lasts only while nobody sells.

**Believing stablecoin means guaranteed.** A stablecoin represents an aim, not a law of physics. In May 2022, the algorithmic stablecoin UST lost its peg and collapsed toward zero within days, erasing tens of billions in value. Even asset-backed coins can fluctuate when banking reserves face disruptions. An indicator, a high yield, or a calm chart does not eliminate issuer risk. Treat stablecoins as useful trading collateral, not as risk-free cash.
