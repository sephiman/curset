# Technical strategy and large migrations

## The Staff role and choosing what to work on
<!-- bullets: 12.5.a -->

Will Larson sorts Staff engineering work into four archetypes and describes the needed but often invisible tasks that keep a team shipping.

:::judgement{source="Will Larson, Staff Engineer: Staff archetypes" ref="staffeng-staff-archetypes#staff-archetypes"}
According to Will Larson, the Tech Lead guides the approach and execution of a particular team, the Architect is responsible for the direction, quality and approach within a critical area, and the Solver digs into arbitrarily complex problems and finds a path forward. The Right Hand extends an executive's attention, borrowing their scope and authority to operate complex organizations. He says his taxonomy aims to be useful more than complete.
:::

:::judgement{source="Will Larson, Staff Engineer: What do Staff engineers actually do?" ref="staffeng-what-staff-do#being-glue"}
According to Will Larson, a core element of successful Staff engineers is doing the needed but often invisible tasks that keep the team moving forward and shipping its work. He writes that this is not glamorous, but that high-impact organizations often have one or more Staff engineers working behind the scenes to expedite the most important work and make sure it gets finished. He credits Tanya Reilly's post Being Glue for the idea.
:::

## Published engineering career ladders
<!-- bullets: 12.5.b -->

GitLab and Dropbox publish their engineering career frameworks. The passages say what the move to Staff changes and what a framework is not.

:::judgement{source="GitLab, IC leadership" ref="gitlab-ic-leadership#engineering-ic-leadership-at-gitlab-going-beyond-senior-level"}
According to the GitLab handbook, Engineering IC Leadership is an alternative career path to Engineering Management, and the move from Senior to Staff changes the day-to-day work and the expectations on individual contributors. It says IC leaders exert technical leverage in their scope of influence, that their impact multiplies with every person they help grow, and that the role stays hands-on technical.
:::

:::judgement{source="Dropbox, Engineering career framework" ref="dropbox-career-framework#what-the-career-framework-is-not"}
According to Dropbox's engineering career framework, it is not a promotion checklist but a way to work out what impact could look like at the next level. Dropbox says it is not an exhaustive list of behaviours either, since each responsibility has three to four key behaviours that serve as a guide, and employees must meet their manager to define impact goals and align on expectations.
:::

## Technical strategy: diagnosis, policy and actions
<!-- bullets: 12.5.c -->

Will Larson describes how to write an engineering strategy and a vision, and when a team needs a strategy.

:::judgement{source="Will Larson, Staff Engineer: Writing engineering strategy" ref="staffeng-engineering-strategy#synthesize-those-five-design-docs-into-a-strategy"}
According to Will Larson, a person writes an engineering strategy by reading five design documents together and pulling out the controversial decisions that appeared in several of them. He says good strategies guide tradeoffs and explain the rationale, while bad strategies state a policy without explanation and so lose their context. He adds that his definition of strategy is the diagnosis and guiding policies of Good Strategy, Bad Strategy, with the coherent action left to the design documents.
:::

:::judgement{source="Will Larson, Staff Engineer: Writing engineering strategy" ref="staffeng-engineering-strategy#extrapolate-five-strategies-into-a-vision"}
According to Will Larson, a person writes an engineering vision by taking five recent strategies and extrapolating their tradeoffs two to three years ahead. He says a useful vision is grounded in the business and its users, optimistic rather than audacious, concrete and specific, and one to two pages long.
:::

:::judgement{source="Will Larson, Staff Engineer: Writing engineering strategy" ref="staffeng-engineering-strategy#when-and-why"}
According to Will Larson, strategies are tools of proactive alignment that let people make quick, confident decisions that might otherwise have cost a week of discussion. He says that if a team has rehashed the same discussion three or four times, it is time to write a strategy.
:::

## Large migrations
<!-- bullets: 12.5.d -->

The sources below describe the strangler fig, branch by abstraction, dual writing and Will Larson's phases for running a migration to completion.

:::judgement{source="Martin Fowler, Strangler Fig" ref="fowler-strangler-fig#banner"}
According to Martin Fowler, the strangler fig approach to legacy modernization replaces a system gradually. It begins with small additions built on top of the legacy code base and moves bits of behaviour into the new code. He says teams often balk at building transitional architecture that goes away after the modernization, and holds that the reduced risk and earlier value outweigh its cost.
:::

:::judgement{source="Martin Fowler, Branch By Abstraction" ref="fowler-branch-by-abstraction#banner"}
According to Martin Fowler, branch by abstraction is a technique for making a large-scale change gradually while the system keeps being released. The team creates an abstraction layer over the current supplier, moves client code to call through it, builds a new supplier behind the same layer and switches clients over one section at a time. Once the old supplier is not needed it is deleted, and the abstraction layer may be deleted too.
:::

:::judgement{source="Jacqueline Xu, Stripe, Online migrations at scale" ref="stripe-online-migrations#a-pattern-for-online-migrations"}
According to Jacqueline Xu of Stripe, in the post Online migrations at scale, the common dual writing pattern for large online migrations has four steps: dual write to the existing and new tables to keep them in sync, change all read paths to read from the new table, change all write paths to write only to the new table, and remove the old data that relies on the outdated data model.
:::

:::judgement{source="Will Larson, Migrations: the sole scalable fix to tech debt" ref="lethain-migrations#derisk"}
According to Will Larson, the first phase of a migration is derisking it as quickly and cheaply as possible: write a design document, show it to the teams that will have the hardest time migrating and test it against the next six to twelve months of roadmap. He advises embedding with the most challenging one or two teams and not starting with the easiest migrations, which can lead to a false sense of security.
:::

:::judgement{source="Will Larson, Migrations: the sole scalable fix to tech debt" ref="lethain-migrations#finish"}
According to Will Larson, the last phase of a migration is deprecating the legacy system, which requires reaching 100% adoption. He says to start by ensuring that all newly written code uses the new approach, for example with a ratchet in the linters, so that progress is made by default. Tracking tickets and a status report to management follow, and the team leading the migration finishes the long tail of unstaffed work itself.
:::

## Build versus buy and technology evaluation
<!-- bullets: 12.5.e -->

Whether to build software or buy a package depends on what the business function is for. Martin Fowler separates utility functions from strategic ones and draws a different conclusion for each.

:::judgement{source="Martin Fowler, Utility Vs Strategic Dichotomy" ref="fowler-utility-vs-strategic#banner"}
According to Martin Fowler, whether software is a utility or a strategic asset depends on whether the underlying business function is a differentiator. In his view, the biggest risk of a utility project is a catastrophic error, so costs should be as low as possible, while the biggest risk of a strategic project is not acting before competitors do.
:::

:::judgement{source="Martin Fowler, Utility Vs Strategic Dichotomy" ref="fowler-utility-vs-strategic#banner"}
According to Martin Fowler, the dichotomy helps decide between custom software and a package. For a utility function he would buy the package and adjust the business process to match it, and he calls heavy customization of such a package just as wasteful. For a strategic function he says the company does not want the same software as its competitors, because that would cripple its ability to differentiate.
:::

## Technical debt
<!-- bullets: 12.5.f -->

Martin Fowler uses debt as a metaphor for the cost of poor internal quality. He describes its interest, how much of the principal to pay down and how to tell prudent debt from reckless debt.

:::judgement{source="Martin Fowler, Technical Debt" ref="fowler-technical-debt#banner"}
According to Martin Fowler, technical debt is a metaphor coined by Ward Cunningham that frames how to deal with cruft, the deficiencies in internal quality that make a system harder to modify and extend. The extra effort needed to add new features is the interest paid on the debt. In his example, a feature takes four days with a clear module structure and six with the cruft, so the two-day difference is the interest.
:::

:::judgement{source="Martin Fowler, Technical Debt" ref="fowler-technical-debt#is-high-quality-software-worth-the-cost"}
According to Martin Fowler, the usual best route is to pay the principal down gradually, spending some extra time removing cruft on each feature that touches an area. He says this naturally concentrates the effort on the areas modified most often. In his account, crufty but stable areas can be left alone, since interest is paid only when the code is modified, while areas of high activity need a zero-tolerance attitude to cruft.
:::

:::judgement{source="Martin Fowler, Technical Debt Quadrant" ref="fowler-technical-debt-quadrant#banner"}
According to Martin Fowler, the useful distinction is not between debt and non-debt but between prudent and reckless debt, and he draws a further distinction between deliberate and inadvertent debt. He writes that a prudent debt taken to reach a release may not be worth paying down if the interest payments are small, such as in a rarely touched part of the code base.
:::

## Estimation and risk in multi-quarter projects
<!-- bullets: 12.5.g -->

Alvidrez says the cost of reliability does not grow linearly, Fowler says the costs of quality cannot be measured objectively and Wright says migration costs are frequently underestimated.

:::judgement{source="Marc Alvidrez, Site Reliability Engineering, ch. 3 Embracing Risk" ref="sre-book-embracing-risk#managing-risk-OKsGh8"}
According to Marc Alvidrez, cost does not increase linearly with reliability, and an incremental improvement in reliability may cost 100 times more than the previous one.
:::

:::judgement{source="Martin Fowler, Technical Debt" ref="fowler-technical-debt#is-high-quality-software-worth-the-cost"}
According to Martin Fowler, none of the costs in the quality trade-off are objectively measurable, because productivity cannot be measured. He says a team can estimate how long a feature takes, how long it would take without the cruft and what removing the cruft costs, but that the accuracy of such estimates is pretty low.
:::

:::judgement{source="Hyrum Wright, Software Engineering at Google, ch. 15 Deprecation" ref="abseil-swe-ch15#why_is_deprecation_so_hardquestion_mark"}
According to Hyrum Wright, migrating to entirely new systems at Google is extremely expensive and the costs are frequently underestimated.
:::

## Deprecating and retiring systems and APIs
<!-- bullets: 12.5.h -->

Hyrum Wright describes why Google deprecates systems and what a deprecation needs to reach its end.

:::judgement{source="Hyrum Wright, Software Engineering at Google, ch. 15 Deprecation" ref="abseil-swe-ch15#why_deprecatequestion_mark"}
According to Hyrum Wright, deprecation starts from the premise that code is a liability, not an asset, since a system has ongoing costs for operations and for keeping up with its ecosystem. He says age alone does not justify deprecation, and that it suits systems that are demonstrably obsolete and have a replacement with comparable functionality. In his account, keeping two systems for the same job lets maintenance costs grow, and the old system impedes the evolution of the new one. He adds that organizations have a limit on how much deprecation work is reasonable to undertake at the same time.
:::

:::judgement{source="Hyrum Wright, Software Engineering at Google, ch. 15 Deprecation" ref="abseil-swe-ch15#advisory_deprecation"}
According to Hyrum Wright, advisory deprecations have no deadline and are not high priority, and he says a warning alone rarely leads teams to migrate.
:::

:::judgement{source="Hyrum Wright, Software Engineering at Google, ch. 15 Deprecation" ref="abseil-swe-ch15#compulsory_deprecation"}
According to Hyrum Wright, compulsory deprecations usually come with a removal deadline and need an enforcement mechanism, and he strongly advocates that a specialized team staffs them through to completion.
:::

:::judgement{source="Hyrum Wright, Software Engineering at Google, ch. 15 Deprecation" ref="abseil-swe-ch15#process_owners"}
According to Hyrum Wright, a deprecation process without explicit owners is unlikely to make meaningful progress however many warnings a system generates. He says the alternatives are to never deprecate anything or to delegate the work to the system's users, which turns it into an advisory deprecation that never finishes.
:::

:::judgement{source="Hyrum Wright, Software Engineering at Google, ch. 15 Deprecation" ref="abseil-swe-ch15#preventing_backsliding"}
According to Hyrum Wright, tooling that prevents new uses of the thing being removed is an often overlooked part of deprecation infrastructure. Without it, he says, deprecation becomes whack-a-mole in which users keep adding uses and the team keeps migrating them.
:::

## Key points

- According to Dropbox, its career framework is not a promotion checklist but a way to work out what impact could look like at the next level.
- According to Will Larson, a strategy uses diagnosis and guiding policies, and a vision extrapolates five strategies two to three years ahead.
- According to Martin Fowler, strangler fig replaces a system gradually and branch by abstraction deletes the old supplier once it is not needed.
- According to Jacqueline Xu, the dual writing pattern has four steps, from dual writes to removing the old data.
- According to Will Larson, a migration ends by reaching 100% adoption, starting with making all new code use the new approach.
- According to Martin Fowler, he would buy a package for a utility function, while a strategic function should not run the same software as its competitors.
- According to Hyrum Wright, a deprecation without explicit owners is unlikely to make progress, and tooling that blocks new uses prevents backsliding.
