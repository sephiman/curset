# Technical strategy and large migrations

## The Staff role and choosing what to work on
<!-- bullets: 12.5.a -->

:::judgement{source="Will Larson, Staff Engineer: Staff archetypes" ref="staffeng-staff-archetypes#staff-archetypes"}
According to Will Larson, the Tech Lead guides the approach and execution of a particular team, the Architect is responsible for the direction, quality and approach within a critical area, and the Solver digs into arbitrarily complex problems and finds a path forward. The Right Hand extends an executive's attention, borrowing their scope and authority to operate complex organizations. He says his taxonomy aims to be useful more than complete.
:::

:::judgement{source="Will Larson, Staff Engineer: Staff archetypes" ref="staffeng-staff-archetypes#right-hand"}
According to Will Larson, the Right Hand is the least common archetype and appears once an organization reaches hundreds of engineers. He writes that the Right Hand's problems are never purely technical and involve the intersection of business, technology, people, culture and process. In his description, the Right Hand dives into a fire, edits the approach, delegates execution and moves on to the next fire, so the joy is working only on essential problems and the tragedy is always being on to the next issue.
:::

:::judgement{source="Will Larson, Staff Engineer: Staff archetypes" ref="staffeng-staff-archetypes#which-is-right-for-you"}
According to Will Larson, a person choosing an archetype should start from the kinds of work that energize them and then look at which roles exist in their company. He says the Tech Lead is the most accessible way to a first Staff role, because every company needs one. In his account, Tech Leads and Architects work with the same people on the same problems for years, while Solvers and Right Hands move from fire to fire and may have a limited sense of community. He notes that in a career of thirty or forty years a person has time to sample every archetype.
:::

:::judgement{source="Michael Wildpaner and others, The Site Reliability Workbook, ch. 18 SRE Engagement Model" ref="sre-workbook-engagement-model#sre-engagement-model"}
According to the authors of the SRE Workbook chapter on the engagement model, a team that knows it cannot cover every service has to decide where to focus its attention to achieve the best results. They write that the model is less effective when the domain is too large and overly complex, which the microservices movement makes more acute.
:::

## Published engineering career ladders
<!-- bullets: 12.5.b -->

:::judgement{source="GitLab, IC leadership" ref="gitlab-ic-leadership#engineering-ic-leadership-at-gitlab-going-beyond-senior-level"}
According to the GitLab handbook, Engineering IC Leadership is an alternative career path to Engineering Management, and the move from Senior to Staff changes the day-to-day work and the expectations on individual contributors. It says IC leaders exert technical leverage in their scope of influence, that their impact multiplies with every person they help grow, and that the role stays hands-on technical. Communication, collaboration and leadership become the new skills that drive the career from then on.
:::

:::judgement{source="GitLab, IC leadership" ref="gitlab-ic-leadership#technical-leverage"}
According to the GitLab handbook, technical leverage is technical work with a multiplicative impact, and it frequently involves activities that are not writing code. The handbook gives examples such as taking part in the architecture process, solving several problems at once by finding what they share, sharing knowledge through training and removing roadblocks in the development process.
:::

:::judgement{source="GitLab, Engineering career framework: Staff" ref="gitlab-engineering-matrix-staff#engineering-function-competencies-staff"}
According to GitLab's career framework for Staff, a staff engineer is expected to show leadership competencies, technical competencies and values alignment, on top of everything expected of a Senior.
:::

:::judgement{source="Dropbox, Engineering career framework" ref="dropbox-career-framework#what-the-career-framework-is-not"}
According to Dropbox's engineering career framework, it is not a promotion checklist but a way to work out what impact could look like at the next level. Dropbox says it is not an exhaustive list of behaviours either, since each responsibility has three to four key behaviours that serve as a guide, and employees must meet their manager to define impact goals and align on expectations.
:::

:::judgement{source="Dropbox, Engineering career framework" ref="dropbox-career-framework#what-s-in-a-career-framework"}
According to the same framework, it has two components. Level expectations define the scope, collaborative reach and levers for impact at every level. Core and craft responsibilities define the key behaviours specific to a role and team.
:::

:::judgement{source="Dropbox, Engineering career framework" ref="dropbox-career-framework#how-to-navigate-this-framework"}
According to the same framework, Dropbox measures the success of its engineers largely on business impact, and a reader should first anchor on creating long-term impact. It then asks the reader to ground themselves in the expectations for their level and team, read the behaviours across the four pillars of results, direction, talent and culture, read the craft expectations and finally meet their manager to set goals for the quarter.
:::

## Technical strategy: diagnosis, policy and actions
<!-- bullets: 12.5.c -->

:::judgement{source="Will Larson, Staff Engineer: Writing engineering strategy" ref="staffeng-engineering-strategy#synthesize-those-five-design-docs-into-a-strategy"}
According to Will Larson, a person writes an engineering strategy by reading five design documents together and pulling out the controversial decisions that appeared in several of them. He says good strategies guide tradeoffs and explain the rationale, while bad strategies state a policy without explanation and so lose their context. He adds that his definition of strategy is the diagnosis and guiding policies of Good Strategy, Bad Strategy, with the coherent action left to the design documents.
:::

:::judgement{source="Will Larson, Staff Engineer: Writing engineering strategy" ref="staffeng-engineering-strategy#synthesize-those-five-design-docs-into-a-strategy"}
According to Will Larson, a strategy document should start where the author is and say specific things, because specific statements create alignment and generic statements create the illusion of it. He asks for opinionated strategies that show their work, so that others can modify them when the context shifts. He says some of the best strategies feel too obvious to write, such as when to write design documents or which databases serve which use cases.
:::

:::judgement{source="Will Larson, Staff Engineer: Writing engineering strategy" ref="staffeng-engineering-strategy#extrapolate-five-strategies-into-a-vision"}
According to Will Larson, a person writes an engineering vision by taking five recent strategies and extrapolating their tradeoffs two to three years ahead. He says a useful vision is grounded in the business and its users, optimistic rather than audacious, concrete and specific, and one to two pages long. He suggests judging a vision by comparing a design document from two years ago with one from last week, and not by the excitement it creates at first.
:::

:::judgement{source="Will Larson, Staff Engineer: Writing engineering strategy" ref="staffeng-engineering-strategy#when-and-why"}
According to Will Larson, strategies are tools of proactive alignment that let people make quick, confident decisions that might otherwise have cost a week of discussion. He says that if a team has rehashed the same discussion three or four times, it is time to write a strategy, and that when the future is too hazy to see which investments are worth making, it is time to write another vision.
:::

## Large migrations
<!-- bullets: 12.5.d -->

:::judgement{source="Martin Fowler, Strangler Fig" ref="fowler-strangler-fig#banner"}
According to Martin Fowler, the strangler fig approach to legacy modernization replaces a system gradually. It begins with small additions built on top of the legacy code base and moves bits of behaviour into the new code. He credits Ian Cartwright, Rob Horn and James Lewis with four activities that the incremental approach needs: understanding the outcomes wanted, deciding how to break the problem into smaller parts, delivering the parts, and changing the organization so this can continue. He says teams often balk at building transitional architecture that goes away after the modernization, and holds that the reduced risk and earlier value outweigh its cost.
:::

:::judgement{source="Martin Fowler, Branch By Abstraction" ref="fowler-branch-by-abstraction#banner"}
According to Martin Fowler, branch by abstraction is a technique for making a large-scale change gradually while the system keeps being released. The team creates an abstraction layer over the current supplier, moves client code to call through it, builds a new supplier behind the same layer and switches clients over one section at a time. He notes that feature flags can run the new supplier in test environments and compare its behaviour with the old one. Once the old supplier is not needed it is deleted, and the abstraction layer may be deleted too.
:::

:::judgement{source="Danilo Sato, Parallel Change (on martinfowler.com)" ref="fowler-parallel-change#banner"}
According to Danilo Sato, parallel change, also known as expand and contract, is a pattern for backward-incompatible changes to an interface that breaks the change into three phases: expand, migrate and contract. He says it is hard to implement a change and update all its usages at the same time, especially for a published interface with many or external clients.
:::

:::judgement{source="Jacqueline Xu, Stripe, Online migrations at scale" ref="stripe-online-migrations#a-pattern-for-online-migrations"}
According to Jacqueline Xu of Stripe, in the post Online migrations at scale, moving millions of objects from one database table to another is difficult but something many companies need to do. It describes a common four-step dual writing pattern for large online migrations: dual write to the existing and new tables to keep them in sync, change all read paths to read from the new table, change all write paths to write only to the new table, and remove the old data that relies on the outdated data model.
:::

:::judgement{source="Jacqueline Xu, Stripe, Online migrations at scale" ref="stripe-online-migrations#part-1-dual-writing"}
According to the same post by Jacqueline Xu, new writes update both stores, and existing objects are copied lazily whenever they are updated before the remaining ones are backfilled. The post suggests slowly ramping up the percentage of objects that get duplicated while watching operational metrics, because of the performance impact of the extra write on the production database. For the backfill, the post finds the objects to migrate with an offline MapReduce job over database snapshots, which has no impact on production, and runs that job again after the migration to check that no objects are missing.
:::

## Build versus buy and technology evaluation
<!-- bullets: 12.5.e -->

:::judgement{source="Martin Fowler, Utility Vs Strategic Dichotomy" ref="fowler-utility-vs-strategic#banner"}
According to Martin Fowler, whether software is a utility or a strategic asset depends on whether the underlying business function is a differentiator. He says the two kinds of project need to be staffed, run and budgeted differently, and that only a few projects are strategic. In his view, the biggest risk of a utility project is a catastrophic error, so costs should be as low as possible, while the biggest risk of a strategic project is not acting before competitors do.
:::

:::judgement{source="Martin Fowler, Utility Vs Strategic Dichotomy" ref="fowler-utility-vs-strategic#banner"}
According to Martin Fowler, the dichotomy helps decide between custom software and a package. For a utility function he would buy the package and adjust the business process to match it, and he calls heavy customization of such a package just as wasteful. For a strategic function he says the company does not want the same software as its competitors, because that would cripple its ability to differentiate. He adds that a strategic activity can become a utility over time.
:::

## Technical debt
<!-- bullets: 12.5.f -->

:::judgement{source="Martin Fowler, Technical Debt" ref="fowler-technical-debt#banner"}
According to Martin Fowler, technical debt is a metaphor coined by Ward Cunningham that frames how to deal with cruft, the deficiencies in internal quality that make a system harder to modify and extend. The extra effort needed to add new features is the interest paid on the debt. In his example, a feature takes four days with a clear module structure and six with the cruft, so the two-day difference is the interest.
:::

:::judgement{source="Martin Fowler, Technical Debt" ref="fowler-technical-debt#is-high-quality-software-worth-the-cost"}
According to Martin Fowler, the usual best route is to pay the principal down gradually, spending some extra time removing cruft on each feature that touches an area. He says this naturally concentrates the effort on the areas modified most often. In his account, crufty but stable areas can be left alone, since interest is paid only when the code is modified, while areas of high activity need a zero-tolerance attitude to cruft.
:::

:::judgement{source="Martin Fowler, Technical Debt" ref="fowler-technical-debt#is-high-quality-software-worth-the-cost"}
According to Martin Fowler, taking on debt deliberately to speed delivery only works while the team stays below the design payoff line, and teams hit that line in weeks rather than months. He suggests asking whether debt was acquired deliberately and whether it is prudent or reckless, which leads to his technical debt quadrant.
:::

## Estimation and risk in multi-quarter projects
<!-- bullets: 12.5.g -->

:::judgement{source="Marc Alvidrez, Site Reliability Engineering, ch. 3 Embracing Risk" ref="sre-book-embracing-risk#managing-risk-OKsGh8"}
According to Marc Alvidrez, cost does not increase linearly with reliability, and an incremental improvement in reliability may cost 100 times more than the previous one. He names two dimensions of cost: redundant machine and compute resources, and the opportunity cost of engineers working on risk reduction instead of features for users. In the SRE approach, the goal is to align the risk a service takes with the risk the business is willing to bear, making it reliable enough but no more reliable than it needs to be.
:::

:::judgement{source="Marc Alvidrez, Site Reliability Engineering, ch. 3 Embracing Risk" ref="sre-book-embracing-risk#measuring-service-risk-XQsyTn"}
According to Marc Alvidrez, SRE focuses on unplanned downtime to make service risk tractable, and expresses risk tolerance as the number of nines of availability, where each additional nine is an order of magnitude improvement toward 100%. He notes that many effects of failure, such as reputational impact and loss of trust, are very hard to measure.
:::

:::judgement{source="Martin Fowler, Technical Debt" ref="fowler-technical-debt#is-high-quality-software-worth-the-cost"}
According to Martin Fowler, none of the costs in the quality trade-off are objectively measurable, because productivity cannot be measured. He says a team can estimate how long a feature takes, how long it would take without the cruft and what removing the cruft costs, but that the accuracy of such estimates is pretty low.
:::

:::judgement{source="Hyrum Wright, Software Engineering at Google, ch. 15 Deprecation" ref="abseil-swe-ch15#why_is_deprecation_so_hardquestion_mark"}
According to Hyrum Wright, migrating to entirely new systems at Google is extremely expensive and the costs are frequently underestimated.
:::

:::judgement{source="Hyrum Wright, Software Engineering at Google, ch. 15 Deprecation" ref="abseil-swe-ch15#why_deprecatequestion_mark"}
According to Hyrum Wright, organizations have a limit on how much deprecation work is reasonable to undertake at the same time, so projects should be chosen with care and then finished.
:::

## Deprecating and retiring systems and APIs
<!-- bullets: 12.5.h -->

:::judgement{source="Hyrum Wright, Software Engineering at Google, ch. 15 Deprecation" ref="abseil-swe-ch15#why_deprecatequestion_mark"}
According to Hyrum Wright, deprecation starts from the premise that code is a liability, not an asset, since a system has ongoing costs for operations and for keeping up with its ecosystem. He says age alone does not justify deprecation, and that it suits systems that are demonstrably obsolete and have a replacement with comparable functionality. In his account, keeping two systems for the same job lets maintenance costs grow, and the old system impedes the evolution of the new one.
:::

:::judgement{source="Hyrum Wright, Software Engineering at Google, ch. 15 Deprecation" ref="abseil-swe-ch15#types_of_deprecation"}
According to Hyrum Wright, deprecation is a continuum that divides into advisory and compulsory.
:::

:::judgement{source="Hyrum Wright, Software Engineering at Google, ch. 15 Deprecation" ref="abseil-swe-ch15#advisory_deprecation"}
According to Hyrum Wright, advisory deprecations have no deadline and are not high priority, and he says a warning alone rarely leads teams to migrate.
:::

:::judgement{source="Hyrum Wright, Software Engineering at Google, ch. 15 Deprecation" ref="abseil-swe-ch15#compulsory_deprecation"}
According to Hyrum Wright, compulsory deprecations usually come with a removal deadline and need an enforcement mechanism, and he strongly advocates that a specialized team staffs them through to completion.
:::

:::judgement{source="Hyrum Wright, Software Engineering at Google, ch. 15 Deprecation" ref="abseil-swe-ch15#process_owners"}
According to Hyrum Wright, a deprecation process without explicit owners is unlikely to make meaningful progress however many warnings a system generates. He says the alternatives are to never deprecate anything or to delegate the work to the system's users, which turns it into an advisory deprecation that never finishes.
:::

:::judgement{source="Hyrum Wright, Software Engineering at Google, ch. 15 Deprecation" ref="abseil-swe-ch15#milestones"}
According to Hyrum Wright, the only milestone of a deprecation can feel like removing the old system, and managers should resist making it the only measurable one. He recommends concrete incremental milestones that are measurable and deliver value to users, such as deleting a key subcomponent.
:::

:::judgement{source="Hyrum Wright, Software Engineering at Google, ch. 15 Deprecation" ref="abseil-swe-ch15#preventing_backsliding"}
According to Hyrum Wright, tooling that prevents new uses of the thing being removed is an often overlooked part of deprecation infrastructure. Without it, he says, deprecation becomes whack-a-mole in which users keep adding uses and the team keeps migrating them. He describes Google surfacing new uses of deprecated symbols at review time and using build visibility lists to block new dependencies.
:::

:::judgement{source="Michael Wildpaner and others, The Site Reliability Workbook, ch. 18 SRE Engagement Model" ref="sre-workbook-engagement-model#phase-5-deprecation"}
According to the authors of the SRE Workbook chapter on the engagement model, once a better replacement system is available, the existing system is closed to new users and all engineering focuses on moving users to the new one. They note that SRE then effectively supports two full systems, so headcount and staffing should be adjusted.
:::
