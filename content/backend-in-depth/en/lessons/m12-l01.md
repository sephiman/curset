# Architecture documentation, decision making and governance

## Architecture decision records and design documents
<!-- bullets: 12.1.a -->

An architecture decision record keeps a decision together with the reasons for it. Nygard's case for it is that a newcomer who does not know the reasons can only accept the decision blindly or change it blindly. A design document sets out the trade-offs before the coding starts, and Google's practices say a change description should say why the change exists.

:::judgement{source="Michael Nygard, Documenting Architecture Decisions" ref="nygard-adr#context"}
According to Michael Nygard, agile methods are not opposed to documentation, only to valueless documentation. He writes that documents that help the team have value only if they are kept up to date, and that large documents are never kept up to date. Small, modular documents have at least a chance of being updated. In his account, the motivation behind a decision is one of the hardest things to track, and a newcomer without it can only blindly accept the decision or blindly change it.
:::

:::judgement{source="Michael Nygard, Documenting Architecture Decisions" ref="nygard-adr#decision"}
According to Michael Nygard, an architecture decision record is a short text file that describes a set of forces and a single decision in response to them. He keeps records for architecturally significant decisions, which he defines as those that affect structure, non-functional characteristics, dependencies, interfaces or construction techniques. His format has a title, a context, a decision, a status and consequences. The context describes the forces at play in value-neutral language. The decision is written in full sentences with active voice, as "We will …". The status is proposed or accepted, and a later record can mark it deprecated or superseded. Records are numbered sequentially, the numbers are never reused, and a reversed decision stays in the repository marked as superseded. He wants each record to be one or two pages long and written as a conversation with a future developer.
:::

:::judgement{source="Michael Nygard, Documenting Architecture Decisions" ref="nygard-adr#decision"}
According to Michael Nygard, the consequences section lists all consequences of the decision, positive, negative and neutral, because all of them affect the team and project in the future.
:::

:::judgement{source="Michael Nygard, Documenting Architecture Decisions" ref="nygard-adr#consequences"}
According to Michael Nygard, the consequences of one record are very likely to become the context of later records. In his view, keeping the records visible lets everyone see the motivation behind previous decisions as the team changes, and shows when it is time to change an old decision.
:::

:::judgement{source="Malte Ubl, Design Docs at Google" ref="industrialempathy-design-docs#design-docs-at-google"}
According to Malte Ubl, design docs are relatively informal documents that the authors of a system write before they start the coding project. A design doc records the high-level implementation strategy and the key design decisions, with the emphasis on the trade-offs considered. Among its functions he lists early identification of design issues, while changes are still cheap, and achieving consensus around a design.
:::

:::judgement{source="Malte Ubl, Design Docs at Google" ref="industrialempathy-design-docs#alternatives-considered"}
According to Malte Ubl, the alternatives-considered section lists alternative designs that would reasonably have achieved similar outcomes, with the focus on the trade-offs each makes. He calls it one of the most important sections, because it shows why the selected design is the best given the project goals.
:::

:::judgement{source="Malte Ubl, Design Docs at Google" ref="industrialempathy-design-docs#when-not-to-write-a-design-doc."}
According to Malte Ubl, writing a design doc is overhead, and the decision comes down to whether the benefits, such as organizational consensus, documentation and senior review, outweigh the extra work. In his account it turns on whether the solution to the design problem is ambiguous. He says a doc that only says how the solution will be implemented, without trade-offs or alternatives, would probably have been better replaced by writing the program.
:::

:::judgement{source="Josef Blake, When Should I Write an Architecture Decision Record?" ref="spotify-adr-when-to-write#tl-dr-have-you-made-a-significant-decision-that-impacts-how-engineers-write-software-write-an-adr"}
According to Josef Blake of Spotify, an architecture decision record is a document that captures a decision, including the context of how it was made and the consequences of adopting it. He says his team usually arrives at its decisions through discussion in RFCs or in engineering meetings.
:::

:::judgement{source="Josef Blake, When Should I Write an Architecture Decision Record?" ref="spotify-adr-when-to-write#proposing-large-changes"}
According to Josef Blake, his mental model for a large change starts from a problem, no blessed solution, a solution in hand and a big change, and says to write an RFC. If the RFC concluded with a solution, he says to write the record.
:::

:::judgement{source="Josef Blake, When Should I Write an Architecture Decision Record?" ref="spotify-adr-when-to-write#backfilling-decisions"}
According to Josef Blake, for a decision that was made or an implicit standard that formed without being documented, his model is to write a record when there is a problem, a blessed solution and no documentation.
:::

:::judgement{source="Google, eng-practices: Writing good CL descriptions" ref="eng-practices-cl-descriptions#writing-good-cl-descriptions"}
According to Google's engineering practices, a change description is a public record of the change and it should say what is being changed and why. The practices ask the author to include decisions made that are not reflected in the source code. They note that reading code may reveal what the software does but not why it exists, which makes it harder for future developers to know whether they can move Chesterton's fence.
:::

## Request-for-comments processes
<!-- bullets: 12.1.a -->

A request-for-comments process gives a larger change a written route to agreement. The Rust project's README describes its process as a consistent and controlled path, so that stakeholders can be confident about the direction of the project. Ubl describes the review of a design document as the point where the experience of the wider organization enters a design.

:::judgement{source="The Rust project, rust-lang/rfcs README" ref="rust-rfcs-readme#top"}
According to the README of the Rust project's RFC repository, the "RFC" (request for comments) process provides a consistent and controlled path for changes to Rust, such as new features, so that all stakeholders can be confident about the direction of the project. Many changes can go through the normal GitHub pull request workflow, while "substantial" changes are put through a design process and need consensus among the Rust community and the sub-teams. To get a major feature added, the author first gets the RFC merged into the RFC repository as a markdown file, and the RFC then becomes "active" and may be implemented. A sub-team member proposes a motion for final comment period with a disposition of merge, close or postpone, and that period lasts ten calendar days so that it is open for at least five business days. The README says being "active" is not a rubber stamp, and that once accepted RFCs should not be substantially changed: more substantial changes should be new RFCs, with a note added to the original.
:::

:::judgement{source="Malte Ubl, Design Docs at Google" ref="industrialempathy-design-docs#review"}
According to Malte Ubl, in the review phase a design doc is shared with a wider audience than its authors and close collaborators, and reviews can add a lot of value but are also a dangerous trap of overhead. A heavy review is a formal design review meeting in which the author presents to an often very senior audience. He says waiting for such meetings can slow development significantly, and that engineers can mitigate this by seeking the most crucial feedback directly and not blocking progress on the wider review. He says a review is an opportunity for the combined experience of the organization to be incorporated into a design, and that its primary value is not that issues get discovered as such but that this happens relatively early in the development lifecycle, when it is still relatively cheap to make changes.
:::

## Building consensus and influencing without authority
<!-- bullets: 12.1.b -->

Google's practices start a disagreement with the two sides trying to agree on the basis of the written guides, and escalate it when that fails. A reviewer whose suggestion is contested begins by asking whether the developer is right. Larson describes Staff engineers as setting direction by working out what the organization needs and as adding the engineering view to decisions while they can still change.

:::judgement{source="Google, eng-practices: The Standard of Code Review" ref="eng-practices-standard#conflicts"}
According to Google's engineering practices, the first step in any conflict on a code review is for the developer and the reviewer to try to reach consensus, based on the written guides. When that is especially difficult, the practices say a face-to-face meeting or video conference can help, and the result should be recorded as a comment for future readers. If that does not settle it, the most common way out is to escalate, often to a broader team discussion, a Technical Lead, a maintainer of the code or an Eng Manager. The practices add that a change should not sit around because the two sides cannot agree.
:::

:::judgement{source="Google, eng-practices: Handling pushback in code reviews" ref="eng-practices-pushback#who_is_right"}
According to Google's engineering practices on pushback, a reviewer whose suggestion is contested should first consider whether the developer is correct, since developers are often closer to the code. If they are, the reviewer says so and lets the issue drop. If they are not, the reviewer explains why the suggestion is right, in a way that shows an understanding of the reply. The practices say improving code health tends to happen in small steps, that it can take a few rounds of explanation, and that the reviewer should stay polite and let the developer know they are heard.
:::

:::judgement{source="Will Larson, Staff Engineer: What do Staff engineers actually do?" ref="staffeng-what-staff-engineers-do#setting-technical-direction"}
According to Will Larson, Staff engineers speak for their companies' technology, which cannot speak for itself, and he compares the work to being a part-time product manager for technology. He writes that the reality of setting technical direction is far more about understanding and solving the real needs of the organization than about prioritizing technology the engineer is personally excited to learn. In his words, in senior positions you are accountable to the business and organization first and yourself second.
:::

:::judgement{source="Will Larson, Staff Engineer: What do Staff engineers actually do?" ref="staffeng-what-staff-engineers-do#providing-engineering-perspective"}
According to Will Larson, Staff-plus engineers are often unexpectedly pulled into the room where a time-sensitive and important decision is being made. He says this lets them inject the engineering context and perspective while it is still possible to change the outcome, and that these brief moments of input are unduly impactful. He adds that they represent the interests of all of engineering, not just their own.
:::

## Governing the lifecycle when teams generate code with AI
<!-- bullets: 12.1.c -->

Morris argues that inspecting every line of generated code makes the human a bottleneck, and proposes that people work on the loop by building the harness around the agent. Böckeler offers a way to decide how much to review, from the probability of error, its impact and how detectable it is. She describes the harness as guides and sensors around the agent.

:::judgement{source="Kief Morris, Humans and Agents in Software Engineering Loops" ref="morris-humans-and-agents-loops#humans-in-the-loop"}
According to Kief Morris, people who stay in the loop act as gatekeepers on the innermost loop where code is generated, for instance by inspecting each line of what an LLM produces. He says this makes them a bottleneck, because agents can generate code faster than humans can manually inspect it.
:::

:::judgement{source="Kief Morris, Humans and Agents in Software Engineering Loops" ref="morris-humans-and-agents-loops#humans-on-the-loop"}
According to Kief Morris, humans can instead work on the loop. They build and maintain the agent's harness, the collection of specifications, quality checks and workflow guidance that control the loops inside the how loop, and he calls this practice harness engineering. When the output is unsatisfactory, working in the loop fixes the artefact, while working on the loop changes the harness that produced it.
:::

:::judgement{source="Birgitta Böckeler, To vibe or not to vibe" ref="bockeler-to-vibe-or-not-to-vibe#banner"}
According to Birgitta Böckeler, how far AI-generated code should be reviewed is not a binary question of vibe coding being good or bad, because the answer depends. Her risk assessment combines three factors: the probability that the AI gets things wrong, the impact if it does, and the detectability of the error.
:::

:::judgement{source="Birgitta Böckeler, To vibe or not to vibe" ref="bockeler-to-vibe-or-not-to-vibe#combining-the-three-a-sliding-scale-of-review-effort"}
According to Birgitta Böckeler, with low probability, low impact and high detectability vibe coding is fine, and as long as things work and she achieves her goal, she does not review the code at all. High probability, high impact and low detectability call for a high level of review, and she says most situations land somewhere in between.
:::

:::judgement{source="Birgitta Böckeler, Harness engineering for coding agent users" ref="bockeler-harness-engineering#FeedforwardAndFeedback"}
According to Birgitta Böckeler, a harness has guides, which are feedforward controls that steer the agent before it acts, and sensors, which are feedback controls that observe after it acts and help it self-correct. She says feedback alone gives an agent that keeps repeating the same mistakes, and feedforward alone gives an agent that encodes rules but never finds out whether they worked.
:::

:::judgement{source="Birgitta Böckeler, Harness engineering for coding agent users" ref="bockeler-harness-engineering#TheRoleOfTheHuman"}
According to Birgitta Böckeler, a harness is an attempt to make explicit what human developer experience brings, and it can only go so far. In her view a good harness should not necessarily aim to eliminate human input but to direct it to where it is most important.
:::

## Key points

- According to Michael Nygard, an architecture decision record is a short text file with a title, a context, a decision, a status and consequences, and a reversed decision stays in the repository marked as superseded.
- According to Malte Ubl, the alternatives-considered section of a design doc is one of the most important, and the decision to write a doc turns on whether the solution is ambiguous.
- According to Josef Blake, a problem with no blessed solution, a solution in hand and a big change calls for an RFC, and an RFC that concluded with a solution calls for a decision record.
- According to Google's engineering practices, review conflicts start with an attempt at consensus based on the written guides and escalate if that fails.
- According to Will Larson, in senior positions an engineer is accountable to the business and organization first and to themselves second.
- According to Kief Morris, humans work on the loop by building the agent's harness instead of inspecting each line.
- According to Birgitta Böckeler, review effort scales with the probability of error, its impact and its detectability.
