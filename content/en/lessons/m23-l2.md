# Multi-timeframe analysis

m23-l1 left you with a style and, quietly, with two charts: a slower frame and a faster one. This
lesson is about what those two charts are to each other. The relationship is not a matter of taste,
and getting it backwards is one of the most expensive habits a beginner can build.



> **A chart has no opinion. It has its frame's opinion.**

Not "the 15-minute chart is unreliable" and not "the daily chart is the truth". Every frame answers,
but each answers a different question, and a question you did not mean to ask gets answered anyway.

## What changes across frames, and what does not

Open the 4-hour chart of a coin, then open the 15-minute chart of the same coin. You have not switched
data sources. You have not gained access to anything the other chart lacked. One 4-hour candle *is*
sixteen 15-minute candles, and the arithmetic is completely mechanical:

- the 4h open is the open of the *first* of the sixteen;
- the 4h close is the close of the *last* of them;
- the 4h high is the highest high *any* of the sixteen reached;
- the 4h low is the lowest low *any* of them reached;
- the 4h volume is their sum.

Aggregation, not new information.

This is the point m29 makes about a single candle, one level of aggregation up. There, a candle was
an aggregate that throws away who was pressing and in what order — the delta OHLCV cannot carry.
Here, a higher-frame candle is an aggregate that throws away the *path* its lower-frame candles took
to get there. A 4h candle closing green tells you sixteen 15m candles ended higher than the first
one opened. It does not tell you the seventh of them collapsed 2% and the twelfth took it all back.

So changing frame is a lossy compression you choose the strength of. Go coarser and you lose detail;
you do not gain wisdom.

### Levels do not move

The consequence people miss: **a level is a price, and a price is the same number on every chart.**
A level at 25,900 (m03-l2, m08-l1) is at 25,900 on the daily, on the 1-hour and on the 1-minute.

What changes is how much noise surrounds it. On the 15m chart, the approach to 25,900 is forty
candles of wobble, three of which push through and come back. Each of those looks, at the moment it
prints, like something happening. On the 4h chart the same stretch is two or three candles with a
wick into the level and a body that never closed beyond it. The level is the same; the
signal-to-noise is radically different.

That is the reason to look at a higher frame at all. Not because it is wiser. Because aggregation
averages the noise out and leaves the level standing.

## The practical hierarchy

The working division of labour is this:

> **The higher frame sets the context. The lower frame sets the execution.**

Context means: which way is this thing going, and which levels matter. Execution means: where exactly
do I get in, and where exactly is the stop. The higher frame tells you *whether* to look for a long at
all; the lower frame tells you *where* the long begins.

This part usually gets taught as mysticism. "Higher timeframes always win" is not a law of markets.
There is no force that makes a big chart more powerful. What there is, is a mechanism:

- A daily level has been watched by more participants for longer. Every trader who looked at that
  chart in the last three months saw the same line, and a good number of them left orders near it.
- A 15-minute level from two hours ago has been seen by whoever was watching a 15-minute chart in
  the last two hours — a far smaller crowd, with far smaller orders.

So a daily level outweighs a 15m level: more resting orders, from more people. It is order-book
arithmetic, not rank. The limit is clear too: a higher frame rules for its job. Ask the daily chart
where to put your stop and it will hand you something 6% away. Nor is that wisdom: it is a chart
being asked a question it does not answer.

## The error this lesson is named for

This trap costs more money than any indicator ever will.

**You find a trend on the lower frame that is a pullback on the higher one.**

The 15-minute chart shows a textbook advance: higher lows, higher highs, a clean sequence anyone
would call an uptrend (m08-l1's sequence of highs and lows, drawn on a fast chart). You go long.
What you did not look at is the 4-hour chart, where those same candles are one pullback candle
inside a downtrend. There it is a bounce inside a structure that is still making lower highs, and
which the sellers who own that structure are waiting to sell into.

::figure{id=fig-m23-timeframes}

Walk the figure, because it is one stretch of price drawn at two resolutions and every number on the
upper panel came out of the lower one. The window opens near 10,400 and the 4-hour chart falls away
to about 8,760. That leg of some 16% is made of lower highs and lower lows, which is what a
downtrend is. Then the last stretch of the 1-hour panel lifts to around 9,460. On that panel alone
it is a rally: several hundred points, higher lows the whole way, the thing a fast chart makes look
like a fresh trend.

On the 4-hour panel those same bars are one green candle. Price is still thousands below where the
leg began. The fall has retraced about 40% of itself and made no higher high at all. And the figure
shows the resolution the exercise version withholds: the downtrend resumes, and the window closes
near 8,360 — below where the whole bounce started.

In general:

> **Trading the lower frame against the higher one is trading the noise against the signal that
> contains it.**

The lower frame was not lying. There was a rally; you could have made money in it if you had been
out again in an hour. What went wrong is that the trade was *sized and held* as though it were a
trend, when it was an interruption inside somebody else's trend. And the thing that ends an
interruption is the trend resuming, at speed, into your position.

:::note{type=warning}
**Looking for the timeframe that agrees.** The failure mode this creates is not a chart error, it is
an m26 error: opening frame after frame until one of them agrees with the position you already hold.
It always works — some frame always agrees — which is exactly why it proves nothing. You choose your
two frames *before* the trade, and you read them in that order, or you are not analysing, you are
looking for agreement.
:::

## Sequence discipline

The order of operations is this:

> **Context above, trigger below. Never the reverse.**

That is the top-down step of the trading plan (m27-l1) stated as a rule about charts. In practice:

1. **Open the higher frame first.** Direction and the levels that matter. If the answer is "no bias"
   — a range, a chart doing nothing — that is a complete answer, and it means there is no trade to
   go looking for below.
2. Then drop to the lower frame and look only for entries *in the direction the higher frame handed
   you.* The lower frame's job is timing, not permission.
3. **Never go back up to justify the trade.** Looking at the higher frame *after* you have found a
   setup you like is looking for the timeframe that agrees with you.

### Which two frames

Your pair comes from your style (m23-l1), because a scalper and a swing trader do not share a
clock:

| Style | Context | Execution |
|---|---|---|
| Scalper | 15m – 1h | 1m – 5m |
| Day trader | 4h – daily | 5m – 15m |
| Swing trader | Weekly – daily | 4h – 1h |

The ratios matter more than the exact rows. A useful pair sits roughly four to six times apart. At
that distance the higher frame smooths the lower one, and the two are still talking about the same
event. A 1-minute chart against a weekly is not multi-timeframe analysis; it is two unrelated
questions.

**Two frames, three at the outside.** Every frame you add is another chart that can disagree. The
reader who watches five of them is not better informed — they are guaranteed to find one that says
whatever they want, which returns you to the warning above.

### And the stop lives in the entry's frame

This connects directly to m22, risk management: you found the context above and the entry below, so
the stop belongs to the frame you entered on. It goes beyond the structure that would invalidate
*this entry* — the swing low the lower frame just made — and the position is then sized from that
distance, as m22 does it.

Putting a 4-hour stop under a 15-minute entry is not caution. It is a 15-minute trade carrying a
4-hour risk. And since size comes from stop distance, it is also a position several times smaller
than the one you thought you were taking, or a loss several times bigger than the one you planned
for. Pick the frame, then keep it for the whole trade.

:::note{type=info}
Higher frames also cost time. A 4-hour candle takes four hours to close, so "wait for the 4h close
to confirm" is a real instruction with a real price: the move may be well underway by then. That is
not an argument for reading a faster chart instead — it is the trade-off the frame pair *is*. You buy
noise reduction with lateness, and every style on the m23-l1 spectrum picks a different exchange rate.
:::
