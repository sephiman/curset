# Oscillators: RSI and MACD

An oscillator is an indicator, derived from price, that moves within a bounded range. It tries to
describe *momentum* — how fast and how one-sidedly price has been moving lately, not where it sits.
The range is fixed: 0–100 for RSI, a zero line for MACD. So you can compare today's reading to last
month's on the same scale, which is the whole appeal. Two are everywhere in crypto: RSI and MACD.
Both are useful once you know what they compute, and both are misread constantly by people who treat
them as buy and sell buttons.

## What RSI actually computes

RSI (Relative Strength Index) compares the average size of recent *up* moves to the average size of
recent *down* moves, over a lookback period (the default is 14 bars). Then it squeezes the result
onto a 0–100 scale. Mechanically it forms a single ratio — RS = average gain ÷ average loss — and
then maps that ratio onto the scale.

:::note{type=info}
RS = average gain over the lookback ÷ average loss over the lookback.

RSI = 100 − 100 ÷ (1 + RS).

If ups and downs are equal, RS = 1 and RSI = 50. If up moves are three times the size of down moves,
RS = 3 and RSI = 100 − 100 ÷ 4 = 75. As losses shrink toward zero, RS explodes and RSI is dragged
toward 100.
:::

- If almost every recent bar closed up, average losses are near zero and RSI pins near 100.
- If almost every recent bar closed down, average gains are near zero and RSI sits near 0.
- When gains and losses are roughly balanced, RSI hovers around the middle, near 50.

So RSI is a *relative* measure, and it is bounded for a mechanical reason. RS can range from 0 to
infinity, and the `100 − 100 ÷ (1 + RS)` mapping folds that whole range neatly into 0–100. It is not
asking "is price high?" — it is asking "how lopsided have the last 14 bars been?"

Worked reading: say the last 14 bars averaged a 0.9% gain on the up bars and a 0.3% loss on the down
bars. Then RS = 0.9 ÷ 0.3 = 3, and RSI = 100 − 100 ÷ (1 + 3) = 75 — comfortably "overbought", purely
because up bars have been three times the size of down bars. That number says *nothing* about
whether 0.9% is a big move, or about price being high or low in absolute terms. It says only that
recent action has leaned up by 3-to-1. The conventional bands are above 70 = overbought and below 30
= oversold. Those two words are where most beginners lose money.

## What MACD actually computes

MACD (Moving Average Convergence Divergence) is built from three pieces:

- The MACD line: the difference between a fast EMA (12) and a slow EMA (26) of price. When the fast
  average is above the slow one the line is positive — price is pulling away from its recent
  baseline.
- The signal line: a 9-period EMA of the MACD line — a smoothed, slower version of it.
- The histogram: the MACD line minus the signal line, drawn as bars. It is the *gap* between the two
  lines.

An EMA weights recent bars more heavily, so the fast (12) EMA reacts to new prices faster than the
slow (26) EMA. The gap between them therefore measures how quickly price is pulling away from its
own slower baseline — a rate of change, not a level. That rate of change is what "momentum" means
here.

Worked reading: in a rally the 12-EMA sits at 30,500 and the 26-EMA at 30,000, so the MACD line is
+500 — price is well above its slower baseline. The signal line (the 9-EMA of that MACD line) lags
at +420, so the histogram is 500 − 420 = +80. If the next bars push the MACD line to +560 while the
signal only crawls to +470, the histogram *grows* to +90: the move is accelerating. If instead the
MACD line stalls at +500 while the signal catches up to +480, the histogram *shrinks* to +20. Price
may still be near its highs, but the push behind it is fading.

Read it as momentum, not as level.

:::note{type=info}
Both indicators are just arithmetic on past prices. They contain no information price does not already
carry — they *repackage* it into a momentum read. That is honestly useful for spotting acceleration
you might miss on the raw candles, and it is also why they can never "predict" anything on their own.
:::

## Reading MACD crossovers

The MACD has two completely different events that both get called "a cross", and conflating them is
the most common way this indicator gets misread. One is a cross of the signal line; the other is a
cross of the zero line.

### The signal-line cross

The signal-line cross is the MACD line crossing its own 9-period EMA — the moment the histogram
flips sign. The signal line is just a lagging average of the MACD line, so this cross has a purely
mechanical meaning. The MACD line crossed above its signal because it turned up, and crossed below
because it turned down. It is a statement about the *last few bars of momentum*.

Worked reading, continuing the numbers above. An uptrend pauses: the 12-EMA slips to 30,360 while
the 26-EMA has caught up to 30,000, so the MACD line has fallen to +360. The signal line is the
9-EMA of the MACD line, still averaging in the bigger readings from the rally. It sits higher at
+392, so the histogram is 360 − 392 = −32. The histogram is *negative in an uptrend*: momentum has
dipped below its own recent average. Two bars later price resumes: the 12-EMA jumps to 30,430 while
the 26-EMA has only crept to 30,020, so the MACD line is back to +410. The signal line averages nine
bars, so two good bars barely move it — it reads +393. The histogram is 410 − 393 = +17: it has
flipped positive. That flip *is* the bullish signal-line cross.

The number that never moved is the MACD line: it stayed positive the whole time (+360, then +410).
The 12-EMA never fell below the 26-EMA.

### The zero-line cross

The zero-line cross is a bigger, higher-order event. The MACD line *is* the fast EMA minus the slow
EMA, so the MACD line at zero means those two averages are equal. The MACD line crossing zero means
the 12-EMA has crossed the 26-EMA. That is m10's golden cross (or death cross), on the 12/26 pair,
reported in the oscillator pane. It marks a change of trend *regime*, not a short-term momentum
shift within one.

Worked reading: a downtrend has the 12-EMA at 29,400 and the 26-EMA at 29,900, so the MACD line
reads −500 — the recent average is decisively below the older one. Price then turns and rallies
hard. The 12-EMA, weighted toward the new prices, climbs faster: at 29,850 against a 26-EMA that has
only reached 29,700, the MACD line is +150. It crossed zero on the way, and what that crossing
records is that the average price of the last 12 bars is now *above* the average of the last 26.

Keep the two straight by what has to happen for each:

- **Signal cross:** the MACD line changed *direction* relative to its own average. Fires often, many
  times inside one trend without the trend ever being in question.
- **Zero cross:** the two EMAs behind the MACD line changed *order*. Later, and it takes a sustained
  move to produce — the same lag m10 showed for any MA cross. It confirms a regime that is already
  underway; it never calls the turn.

In a clean reversal you see them in that order: the signal cross first, then — if the move is real —
the zero cross some bars later. That ordering is why the signal cross is the earlier, noisier clue and
the zero cross the later, heavier confirmation.

### The histogram shrinking before a cross

You do not have to wait for the flip. The histogram is the *gap* between the two lines, so a
shrinking histogram is the cross approaching: the histogram reaching zero and the cross happening
are the same event.

Play it out. The MACD line stalls at +500 while the signal keeps catching up: the histogram prints
+80, then +50, then +20. Price may still be grinding to new highs the whole time — but the bars
behind it are getting shorter. If the MACD line stays flat while the signal keeps climbing, the next
flip is arithmetic, not opinion. Price making new highs while the histogram makes smaller ones is a
MACD-histogram divergence. It is the same disagreement between price and momentum that m12 builds
on, caught one step earlier, at the histogram rather than the swing highs. It is a clue that the
move is decelerating.

### A crossover is not a standalone signal

Everything above describes what a cross *computes*. What it is *worth* depends entirely on where it
happens — and this is the same point m08-l2 makes about a candle and m13 makes about a fib level.

::figure{id=fig-m11-cross-in-trend-vs-range}

- The same bullish signal-line cross inside an established uptrend is *continuation* evidence. A
  pullback turned the histogram negative, and that pullback is now over. The trend, intact the whole
  time, is resuming, and the cross tells you when. On the left panel this happens several times in
  one trend without the trend ever being in doubt. The cross is worth something there because the
  trend is doing the work and the cross is only timing it.
- The same bullish signal-line cross inside a range is *whipsaw*. In a sideways market the MACD line
  saws back and forth across zero, so crosses fire again and again. Each one looks exactly like the
  one on the left, and none of them leads anywhere. This is m10's whipsaw warning about MA crosses
  in a range, since the MACD *is* two moving averages.

So: a crossover is not a standalone signal, any more than a level or a divergence is. Read the
context first — trend or range, and which side of zero the MACD line is on — and only then ask what
the cross adds.

## The overbought/oversold lie

Beginners learn "RSI over 70 = overbought = sell" and "under 30 = oversold = buy", and then bleed
money to it for months.

**In a strong trend, an oscillator can stay pinned at its extreme for a very long time.** A market in
a powerful uptrend will push RSI above 70 and *keep it there* for days or weeks, printing new highs
the whole way. Every bar that "should" have been the reversal was just another leg up.

::figure{id=fig-m11-overbought-lie}

The chart above is this trap. RSI vaults above 70 near the *start* of the move and stays there,
printing overbought reading after overbought reading while price climbs the entire time. Overbought
does not mean expensive and it does not mean sell — it means momentum has been strongly one-sided.
In a trend that is what you would expect, and it is often a sign of *strength*, not exhaustion. The
same is true upside-down: a hard downtrend parks RSI below 30 and keeps it there.

## The overbought reflex

"RSI is over 70, so I should sell." It is one of the most repeated sentences in retail technical
analysis, and it is not a trade — it is a way to fight a trend and get run over.

Play it out with numbers. A low-cap alt rips from 1.00 to 1.60 in two weeks; RSI crosses 70 at
around 1.15 and never looks back, sitting between 78 and 85 the whole climb. A beginner following
the rule shorts at 1.20 ("it's overbought"), gets stopped out at 1.28. It shorts again at 1.35,
stopped at 1.44. It shorts a third time at 1.50 with a bigger size to "make it back", and the push
to 1.60 takes the account. Every entry was "correct" by the RSI rule.

The reflex runs in reverse just as expensively — going long into a strong downtrend because "RSI is
oversold" hands your stop to the trend.  An extreme reading tells you the move is one-sided; it
never tells you the move is about to end. The reading was never *wrong*: it truthfully said the move
was lopsided. It just never said the move was *done*.

## Nothing resets the oscillator

Crypto makes this trap worse, not better.

- **24/7 markets don't reset.** A stock gets a daily close and an overnight gap that let momentum
  cool off. A crypto perpetual trades every hour of every day. A parabolic move can hold RSI above
  80 for a week straight with no session break to interrupt it.
- **Thin alt books pin the extreme longer.** In a low-cap alt, a modest amount of buying moves price
  a long way. So a narrative-driven rip can keep RSI at an extreme far higher and far longer than
  any large-cap would. Those are the conditions that trap the "sell the overbought" reflex.
- **Funding is the more honest exhaustion clue.** If you want to know whether an up-move is
  overheating, watch funding rather than RSI. In these euphoric runs funding turns sharply positive
  (longs paying shorts to keep the position open). *That* crowding is a real sign of a stretched
  trade in a way that "RSI is 82" is not.
- **The reversal, when it comes, is violent.** The same thin book that let price levitate makes the
  eventual drop brutal. The trader who was "right" about overbought a week too early is rarely still
  solvent to enjoy being right.

## So what do you actually do with them?

- Use RSI to describe context, not to trigger entries. Overbought inside a range is meaningful;
  overbought in a rip-roaring trend is normal.
- Watch the MACD histogram for shifts in momentum — a shrinking histogram warns that a move is
  decelerating before price turns, and warns you a cross is coming. That is a clue, not a command.
- **Weigh a MACD cross by which line was crossed and by the regime it happened in.** A signal-line
  cross is a short-term momentum shift. A zero-line cross is the 12- and 26-EMA changing order, a
  regime change. Either one in a trend is confirmation; either one in a range is whipsaw.
- Treat crosses and band-crossings as *confirmation for a thesis you already hold* from structure
  and levels, never as standalone signals.
